# ---------- L1 W1 Cells ----------
One hemocytometer square has a volume of {{c1::6.25 nL}}, and on these microscopes one quadrant is {{c2::12 squares}}, which equals {{c3::75 nL}} ||| 12 times 6.25 nL is 75 nL. You need the 75 nL figure for every counting calculation. ~~~ A quadrant holds 75 nL: think "75 in a quad."
To get cells per mL from a hemocytometer, take the average quadrant count, divide by {{c1::75 nL}}, then multiply by {{c2::1,000,000}} (the number of nL in one mL) ||| Shortcut: cells per mL equals average count times 13,333. One cell per 75 nL quadrant is 1 divided by 75 times 1,000,000, which is about 13,333 per mL. ~~~ Count, divide by 75, blow up by a million.
The North-West counting rule: cells on the {{c1::top and left}} lines are counted in, cells on the {{c2::bottom and right}} lines are counted out ||| This stops you double-counting cells that sit on grid lines between squares. ~~~ Read the square like a page: start top-left (yours), run off bottom-right (next square's).

# ---------- L2 W2 Microscopy ----------
A fluorophore absorbs light at one wavelength (called {{c1::excitation}}) and emits light at a {{c2::longer}} wavelength (called emission), because some energy is lost as heat ||| The gap between the two peaks is the Stokes shift. Emission is always at longer wavelength, never shorter. ~~~ Toll road: drive in with a full tank (blue), pay the toll inside, leave with less (redder).
On an excitation-emission plot, the curve at the {{c1::shorter}} wavelength is excitation and the curve at the {{c2::longer}} wavelength is emission ||| You can always tell them apart because emission sits to the right of excitation on a wavelength axis. ~~~ Left is what goes in, right is what comes out.
A fluorescence image is really {{c1::monochrome}} data at each wavelength, and the colour you see is {{c2::assigned}} afterward ||| Each pixel holds a number for light intensity. You choose display colours, as long as you declare which is which. Colour-blind-friendly picks are magenta, yellow and cyan. ~~~ The camera sees grey numbers; you paint them.
RNA differs from DNA in that it is {{c1::single-stranded}}, uses {{c2::uracil}} instead of thymine, and has a {{c3::2' OH}} group on its sugar ||| It also folds into complex 3D shapes. The 2' OH makes RNA less stable than DNA. ~~~ RNA is a Restless single strand: You (U) not Thymine, plus an extra OH handle.

# ---------- L3 W3-4 Myocardin ----------
A7r5 cells come from rat {{c1::aortic smooth muscle}}, so they are vascular smooth muscle cells, and they have {{c2::fibroblast-like}} morphology ||| They are embryonic and suitable for transfection. Fibroblast-like means flat and spindly, not the tight contractile shape of mature muscle. ~~~ A7r5 = A Rat's aorta, 5 letters for the 5 facts: rat, aortic, smooth muscle, embryonic, transfectable.
Myocardin is a transcriptional {{c1::coactivator}}: it does not bind DNA itself, but binds the transcription factor {{c2::SRF}} sitting on a {{c3::CArG box}} ||| Together they recruit RNA polymerase II and strongly transcribe smooth muscle genes. ~~~ SRF is the parked car; myocardin is the driver who floors it.
GFP is used as a {{c1::reporter}}: it glows green with no stain, marking cells that {{c2::took up the plasmid}} ||| It lets you calculate transfection efficiency and pick transfected cells for actin analysis. pEGFP (GFP only) is the control for myocardin's effect. ~~~ GFP is the green name-tag for transfected cells.
Transfection efficiency equals {{c1::GFP-positive cells}} divided by {{c2::total cells (Hoechst nuclei)}}, times 100 ||| You count green cells for the numerator and all nuclei for the denominator. ~~~ Green over blue, times a hundred.

# ---------- L4 W5 RNA ----------
The whole BPK 408W project asks what happens when you overexpress {{c1::myocardin}} in cultured {{c2::A7r5 smooth muscle}} cells ||| Myocardin should push the cells toward a contractile smooth muscle phenotype, seen as more organized actin and more contractile-gene mRNA. ~~~ Myocardin Makes Muscle.
The RNA spin column steps in order are {{c1::lyse, bind, wash, elute}}, and every step is add liquid, {{c2::centrifuge}}, deal with the flow-through ||| Lyse breaks cells and shears DNA; bind sticks RNA to silica; wash rinses off junk; elute releases RNA. ~~~ Lazy Bees Wash Early: Lyse, Bind, Wash, Elute.
During the bind and wash steps the RNA is {{c1::on the silica column}}, so you discard the flow-through, but during elution the RNA is {{c2::in the flow-through}}, so you keep it ||| "Know where your RNA is at all times." Tossing the final flow-through tosses your sample. ~~~ Drive-through car wash: the car stays on the track until the exit gate (elute).
In total RNA, the most abundant type is {{c1::rRNA}}, but the type you care about for this project is {{c2::mRNA}} ||| rRNA is about 80 percent of total RNA. You want mRNA of genes myocardin affects, which is only a few percent. ~~~ Ribosomes are loud (abundant), messages are what you actually read.
The A260/A280 ratio checks RNA for {{c1::protein contamination}}, and pure RNA sits around {{c2::2.0}} ||| Nucleic acids absorb at 260 nm, proteins at 280 nm. A low ratio means protein or phenol contamination. ~~~ 260 is the RNA's number; 280 is the protein's. Clean RNA scores a 2.
A denaturing gel adds a chemical that {{c1::unfolds the RNA's 3D shape}}, and you run a {{c2::ladder}} so you can identify band sizes ||| Without denaturing, folded RNA migrates by shape as well as size, so bands look wrong. ~~~ Denature = flatten the origami so size alone decides the race.
Reverse transcription copies RNA into {{c1::cDNA}} using the enzyme {{c2::reverse transcriptase}}, because PCR enzymes can only copy DNA ||| Primers anneal around 25 C, then the enzyme extends at 46 C. ~~~ RT writes a DNA backup of the RNA message.
Poly-T (oligo-dT) primers select for {{c1::mRNA}} because they bind the {{c2::poly-A tail}}, while random primers bind everything ||| Random primers help capture long mRNAs fully but also copy tRNA and rRNA. ~~~ T sticks to A: oligo-dT grabs the A-tail that only mRNAs have.
You measure RNA concentration before reverse transcription so you can load the {{c1::same amount}} of RNA into every reaction, keeping the comparison {{c2::fair}} ||| Otherwise a condition could look like it has more of every transcript just because more RNA went in. ~~~ Same input, fair fight.
The plasmid pMYOCD-IRES-GFP makes {{c1::two separate proteins}} from one mRNA because an {{c2::IRES}} lets a ribosome start again in the middle ||| So a green (GFP) cell is also a myocardin-expressing cell. IRES is internal ribosome entry site. ~~~ IRES is a second front door halfway down the hall.

# ---------- L5 W6 Staining ----------
In the staining protocol you must {{c1::permeabilize}} before phalloidin, because phalloidin can't cross an {{c2::intact membrane}} ||| Order: fix, wash, permeabilize, Hoechst, wash, phalloidin, wash. Washes remove unbound dye that would cause background. ~~~ Open the doors before you paint the inside of the car.
Direct staining means the dye binds the target itself (like {{c1::Hoechst and phalloidin}}), while immunostaining is {{c2::indirect}}, using a primary then a fluorescent secondary antibody ||| Immunostaining is more flexible but has more steps, washes and background. GFP needs neither; it is natural fluorescence. ~~~ Direct = dye grabs it; immuno = antibody relay race.
A pixel that maxes out at {{c1::255}} is called {{c2::saturated (overexposed)}}, and that information is {{c3::lost for good}} ||| Every brighter structure also reads 255, so you can't tell them apart. Slightly underexpose instead; a dim image can be brightened later without losing data. ~~~ 255 is a speedometer that tops out: 260 and 400 both read max.
To make images comparable across conditions, use {{c1::identical exposure and gain}} settings, set exposure so nothing is {{c2::saturated}}, and pick fields {{c3::without bias}} ||| Take several fields per condition and save raw files. Brightness differences should come from the cells, not the settings. ~~~ Same camera, no clipping, no cherry-picking.

# ---------- L6 W7 ddPCR ----------
Measuring DNA by spectrophotometry after RT is useless because the tube still has {{c1::RNA, primers and nucleotides}}, which all absorb at {{c2::260 nm}} ||| The reading can't tell cDNA from everything else, so the number means nothing. ddPCR measures the specific cDNA instead. ~~~ Everything in the tube shouts at 260; you can't hear just the cDNA.
End-point PCR is not quantitative because by the end the reaction hits a {{c1::plateau}}, so tubes with different starting amounts end up {{c2::the same}} ||| Reagents run out and the enzyme tires, so the exponential phase ends. You can't work backward from the plateau. ~~~ A full stadium looks the same whether people came at noon or 6pm.
ddPCR splits the reaction into thousands of {{c1::droplets}}, each getting zero or one target, and counts {{c2::positive}} (bright) versus negative (dim) droplets ||| A droplet with target amplifies to plateau and the dsDNA dye makes it bright; an empty droplet stays dim. ~~~ Egg carton for molecules: count the cups whose brake lights come on.
ddPCR gives an {{c1::absolute}} count because the number comes from {{c2::how many droplets are positive}}, not from brightness ||| A droplet that started with one copy and one that started with three end up about equally bright at plateau, so brightness can't carry the count. ~~~ You count lit cups, not how bright each one is.
The ddPCR software uses {{c1::Poisson}} statistics to correct for droplets that got more than one target, using the fraction of {{c2::negative}} droplets ||| Average copies per droplet equals minus the natural log of the fraction negative. If there are no negatives, the math breaks. ~~~ Poisson needs some empties to do its counting trick.
If every ddPCR droplet is positive, the reaction is {{c1::saturated}} and {{c2::cannot be quantified}}, so you should {{c3::dilute}} the cDNA and rerun ||| With no negative droplets, Poisson can't tell 3 copies per droplet from 300. "All blue" is a failure, not a success. ~~~ All lit = you've lost the count; water it down.
A housekeeping gene is expressed at a {{c1::steady level}} regardless of treatment, so dividing target copies by housekeeping copies removes {{c2::technical variation}} ||| It controls for differences in cell number, RNA input and RT efficiency, which raise all genes equally. ~~~ Divide sales by walk-in customers before you judge the dealership.
In this course the housekeeping genes are {{c1::GAPDH, rTbp1 and beta-actin1}}, and the contractile genes are {{c2::alpha-SMA, SM22-alpha, CNN and MHC}} ||| CNN is calponin, MHC is smooth muscle myosin heavy chain. Beta-actin may not stay steady in an actin experiment, so check your data. ~~~ Housekeepers keep the lights on; contractile genes do the squeezing.
You choose the housekeeping gene whose copies change {{c1::least}} across the DM, pMyo and pEGFP conditions, and that also gives {{c2::clean ddPCR data}} ||| If a housekeeping gene moves with treatment, normalizing to it adds error instead of removing it. ~~~ Pick the flattest line as your yardstick.
Positive droplets from one primer set can be {{c1::brighter}} than another's because amplitude depends on {{c2::product length and amplification efficiency}}, not starting copy number ||| A longer product binds more dsDNA dye, so its positive droplets glow brighter. This is how you can tell mixed-up primer sets apart. ~~~ Bigger product soaks up more dye, shines brighter.

# ---------- L7 W8 Analysis ----------
Hoechst 33342 is a {{c1::DNA}} stain, excites near 350 nm and emits near 461 nm, so it shows in the {{c2::blue}} channel ||| It binds double-stranded DNA, so it marks the nucleus and lets you count every cell. ~~~ Hoechst = Home base (nucleus), blue.
Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) binds {{c1::carbohydrates}} on the cell surface, so it stains the {{c2::plasma membrane}}, and it shows green ||| Excitation near 495 nm, emission near 519 nm. The membrane surface is coated in sugar chains that WGA grabs. ~~~ WGA wraps the outer Wall; sugar coat.
Phalloidin binds {{c1::F-actin}} (the filament form of actin) and is usually linked to a {{c2::red}} fluorophore ||| It shows the shape of the cytoskeleton. Used in Week 6 staining. ~~~ PhallOIDin finds the actin rOADs (filaments), in red.
In the Week 6 three-colour images, blue is {{c1::DNA (Hoechst)}}, green is {{c2::GFP}}, and red is {{c3::F-actin (phalloidin)}} ||| Blue counts all cells, green marks transfected cells, red shows actin shape. ~~~ Brain blue, skin green, skeleton red.
On a gel of total RNA you expect the {{c1::28S and 18S rRNA}} bands, with the 28S about {{c2::twice}} as bright, and intact RNA shows {{c3::no smear}} ||| You infer mRNA integrity from rRNA integrity, because thousands of different mRNAs blur into faint background and can't be seen individually. ~~~ 28 is bigger than 18 and twice as bright; a smear means it's been chewed.
A ddPCR well with no primers (the no-primer control) should show {{c1::only negative droplets}}, which tells you what {{c2::no product}} looks like ||| It checks for contamination or primer-free signal and sets the baseline fluorescence of a negative droplet. ~~~ No primers, no product: the honest zero.
When improving the draft ddPCR bar graph, drop the bars for the housekeeping gene normalized to {{c1::itself}} (always 1.0), use {{c2::real gene names}}, and group bars so conditions sit side by side ||| Also add a legend, error bars for replicates, and consider showing pMyo relative to the pEGFP control. ~~~ A gene over itself is always 1: cut the bar that says nothing.
"What do the data show" asks what you can {{c1::see in the plot}}, while "what do the data mean" asks what it says about the {{c2::cells}} ||| Show: two bands, rain, threshold position. Mean: which gene was expressed, whether the control worked. These are two separate answers. ~~~ Show is the eyes; mean is the brain.
