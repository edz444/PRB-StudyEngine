## Calculations: every number on the quiz {#l8}

:::story
The quiz has calculation questions, and they are the easiest marks to lock in, because the method never changes. This unit drills every calculation type: the formula, a worked example, one to try yourself, and a fast way to check your answer.
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Every calculation here is the same shape: write the formula, put in your numbers with their units, do the arithmetic, and sanity-check the size of the answer. The units are your safety net; if they don't cancel to what you want, you set it up wrong.

:::hook
Formula, numbers with units, arithmetic, sanity-check. Four steps, every time.
:::

### Hemocytometer: cells per mL and seeding volume

:::key
- Turn quadrant counts into a concentration in cells per mL
- Find the volume that seeds a target number of cells
- Sanity-check the final volume
:::

The short version: Average the counts, divide by the volume to get cells per mL, then divide the cells you want by that concentration to get the volume.

The words you need. A quadrant is the block of 12 squares you count, and it holds 75 nL (nanolitres). There are 1,000,000 nL in 1 mL.

Formula: cells/mL = (average count ÷ 75 nL) × 1,000,000. Then volume to seed = cells wanted ÷ cells/mL.

Worked example. Counts are 87, 91, 83, 85. Average = (87+91+83+85) ÷ 4 = 86.5. Concentration = 86.5 ÷ 75 × 1,000,000 = 1.153 × 10⁶ cells/mL. To seed 400,000 cells: 400,000 ÷ 1,153,333 = 0.347 mL, which is 347 µL.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. One cell per 75 nL quadrant equals 13,333 cells/mL, so cells/mL ≈ average count × 13,333. Sanity-check: a volume seeded into a small dish should be tens to a few hundred µL. If you get 0.3 µL or 3,000 mL, you slipped a factor of 1000 somewhere.
:::

Try it. Counts are 120, 116, 124, 120. What volume seeds 500,000 cells?

Answer. Average = 120. Concentration = 120 × 13,333 = 1.6 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Volume = 500,000 ÷ 1,600,000 = 0.3125 mL = 312.5 µL.

### Dilutions with C1V1 = C2V2

:::key
- Use C1V1 = C2V2 to hit a target concentration or volume
- Load the same amount of RNA into every reaction
:::

The short version: The amount of stuff stays the same when you dilute, so starting concentration times starting volume equals final concentration times final volume.

The words you need. C1 and V1 are the concentration and volume you start with (the stock). C2 and V2 are what you want to end with. Solve for whichever one is missing.

Worked example. You have RNA at 400 ng/µL and want 20 µL at 50 ng/µL for reverse transcription. V1 = C2V2 ÷ C1 = (50 × 20) ÷ 400 = 2.5 µL of RNA, topped up with 17.5 µL of water to reach 20 µL.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. Diluting always means a bigger volume and a smaller concentration. If your "start" volume comes out larger than your final volume, you flipped the formula. To load equal RNA into every reaction, pick the lowest sample concentration as your target so every tube can reach it.
:::

Try it. DNA stock is 250 ng/µL. You want 10 µL at 25 ng/µL. How much stock?

Answer. V1 = (25 × 10) ÷ 250 = 1 µL of stock, plus 9 µL water.

### Mass and amount: ng, µg, and stock volumes

:::key
- Convert nanograms to micrograms
- Find the volume of stock that gives a wanted mass
:::

The short version: Divide nanograms by 1000 to get micrograms, and divide the mass you want by the stock concentration to get the volume.

The words you need. 1 µg (microgram) = 1000 ng (nanograms). Concentration is mass per volume, so volume = mass ÷ concentration.

Worked example. The transfection cocktail uses 500 ng of plasmid. That is 500 ÷ 1000 = 0.5 µg. If your plasmid stock is 1000 ng/µL, the volume you pipette is 500 ng ÷ 1000 ng/µL = 0.5 µL.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. Write the units next to every number and cancel them: ng ÷ (ng/µL) leaves µL, which is a volume, so you know the setup is right. If the units leave you with ng² or 1/µL, you divided the wrong way.
:::

Try it. You need 300 ng of DNA from a 150 ng/µL stock. What volume?

Answer. 300 ng ÷ 150 ng/µL = 2 µL.

### Mastermix scaling

:::key
- Scale a single-reaction recipe up to many wells
- Add extra so you don't run short
:::

The short version: Multiply each ingredient's single-reaction volume by the number of reactions, plus one extra reaction's worth to cover pipetting loss.

The words you need. A mastermix is one big batch of the shared reagents, so every well gets the same mix and you pipette fewer times. Pipetting loss is the little bit left in tips and tubes.

Worked example. One reaction needs 0.75 µL Lipofectamine in 25 µL Opti-MEM. For 6 wells, make 7 reactions' worth (n+1): 0.75 × 7 = 5.25 µL Lipofectamine and 25 × 7 = 175 µL Opti-MEM.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. Always make n+1 (one extra reaction) so the last well isn't short. Quick check: the total mastermix volume should equal the per-reaction volume times the number of reactions you made.
:::

Try it. A reaction uses 1 µL P3000. You have 10 wells. How much P3000 for the mastermix?

Answer. Make 11 reactions: 1 × 11 = 11 µL P3000.

### Agarose gel percentage

:::key
- Turn a gel percentage into grams of agarose
:::

The short version: A percentage gel is grams of agarose per 100 mL of buffer, so mass = (percent ÷ 100) × volume in mL.

The words you need. A 0.6% gel means 0.6 g of agarose in every 100 mL. Lower percent means a looser gel for big molecules like rRNA.

Worked example. For a 0.6% gel in 50 mL: mass = (0.6 ÷ 100) × 50 = 0.3 g of agarose.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. Percent is grams per 100 mL, so for 100 mL the grams equal the percent. Scale from there: half the volume, half the grams.
:::

Try it. How much agarose for a 1% gel in 200 mL?

Answer. (1 ÷ 100) × 200 = 2 g.

### Transfection efficiency

:::key
- Work out the percent of cells that took up the plasmid
- Average across fields
:::

The short version: Transfection efficiency is green cells divided by total cells, times 100.

The words you need. Green cells are GFP-positive (they expressed the plasmid). Total cells are all the Hoechst-stained nuclei. A field is one microscope view.

Formula: efficiency (%) = (GFP-positive nuclei ÷ total nuclei) × 100.

Worked example. A field has 182 nuclei and 13 are green: 13 ÷ 182 × 100 = 7.14%. Average three fields (7.14%, 9.76%, 11.26%): mean = 9.39%.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. The answer must be between 0 and 100%. If you get more than 100%, you divided total by green instead of green by total. Averaging the three field percentages is not the same as pooling all nuclei, so say which you did.
:::

Try it. A field has 200 nuclei, 40 green. What is the efficiency?

Answer. 40 ÷ 200 × 100 = 20%.

### Mean, standard deviation and SEM

:::key
- Tell SD and SEM apart
- Compute SEM from SD
:::

The short version: The mean is the average, the standard deviation (SD) measures spread, and the standard error (SEM) is SD divided by the square root of n.

The words you need. n is the number of replicates. SD describes how scattered the data are. SEM describes how precisely you know the mean, and it is always smaller than SD.

Formula: SEM = SD ÷ √n.

Worked example. Three fields, SD = 2.08%, n = 3. √3 ≈ 1.73, so SEM = 2.08 ÷ 1.73 = 1.20%.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. SEM is always smaller than SD, and it shrinks as n grows. For n = 3, SEM ≈ 0.58 × SD. If your SEM comes out bigger than your SD, you multiplied by √n instead of dividing.
:::

Try it. SD = 6, n = 4. What is the SEM?

Answer. √4 = 2, so SEM = 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

### ddPCR: Poisson and normalization

:::key
- Get average copies per droplet from the negative fraction
- Normalize a target gene to a housekeeping gene
:::

The short version: Average copies per droplet is minus the natural log of the negative fraction, and normalized expression is the target gene divided by a housekeeping gene from the same sample.

The words you need. The negative fraction is negative droplets divided by all droplets. Normalizing cancels technical differences because the housekeeping gene rises and falls with them too.

Formulas: λ = −ln(N_negative ÷ N_total). Normalized expression = target copies ÷ housekeeping copies.

Worked example. 15,000 of 20,000 droplets are negative: fraction = 0.75, λ = −ln(0.75) = 0.29 copies per droplet. Normalizing α-SMA: pMyo reads 260 copies and GAPDH 1400, so 260 ÷ 1400 = 0.186; pEGFP reads 150 and 1600, so 150 ÷ 1600 = 0.094. pMyo is 0.186 ÷ 0.094 ≈ 2 times the control.

:::easy
Hack and double-check. If every droplet is positive, the negative fraction is 0 and ln(0) is undefined, so you cannot get a number; dilute and rerun. After normalizing, a raw jump that is matched by the same jump in the housekeeping gene means no real change.
:::

Try it. 18,000 of 20,000 droplets are negative. What is λ?

Answer. Fraction = 0.9, λ = −ln(0.9) = 0.105 copies per droplet.

### How to figure it out: pick the right formula {#howto-l8}

Match the question to its formula:

1. Counting cells in a grid, or seeding a dish? Hemocytometer: average, divide by 75 nL, times a million.
2. Hitting a target concentration or equal input? C1V1 = C2V2.
3. Turning a mass into a pipette volume? Volume = mass ÷ concentration (and ng ÷ 1000 = µg).
4. Making enough mix for many wells? Mastermix: per-reaction volume × (wells + 1).
5. Pouring a gel? Mass = (percent ÷ 100) × mL.
6. Percent of green cells? Efficiency = green ÷ total × 100.
7. Spread or precision? SD for spread, SEM = SD ÷ √n for precision.
8. ddPCR copies or comparing conditions? λ = −ln(negative fraction); normalized = target ÷ housekeeping.

:::hook
Write units on every number and cancel them. The units tell you if the formula is upside down before you ever reach for a calculator.
:::

### Reading her questions on this lecture {#read-l8}

| If the question says... | She's testing... | How you'd get tricked |
|---|---|---|
| 'show your work' | the full setup, not just the answer | writing only the final number |
| 'seed X cells' | hemocytometer then volume | forgetting to average, or the nL to mL step |
| 'equal RNA input' | a C1V1 = C2V2 dilution | topping up to the wrong final volume |
| 'what is the efficiency' | green over total times 100 | an answer above 100% |
