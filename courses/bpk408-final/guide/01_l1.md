### Why grow cells in a dish?

:::key
- List two advantages and two disadvantages of cell culture
- Explain why a cell line can behave unlike the original tissue
:::

The short version: Cell culture gives you total control and cheap replicates, but a dish is not a body, so cells drift.

Cell culture means keeping living cells alive and dividing in a plastic dish with nutrient liquid called medium. You work with mammalian cells, but not whole mammals.

The slide asks you for advantages and disadvantages. On the plus side, you control everything (temperature, nutrients, what you add), cells are cheap and fast to grow, you get many identical replicates, and you avoid animal experiments for each test. On the minus side, a dish is not a body. Cells lose the signals they'd get from neighbouring tissues, blood flow and hormones, and long-term cell lines drift away from how the original cells behaved. A7r5 cells are a good example of that drift (they look fibroblast-like, not like tight contractile muscle).

:::hook
A cell line is a racing car on a dyno. You can test every part in perfect control, but the dyno isn't a real track, so results still need a reality check in tissue.
:::

### Meet A7r5 cells

:::key
- Name where A7r5 cells come from and what type they are
- Recall the four A7r5 facts
- Avoid the rat-vs-human and muscle-type traps
:::

The short version: A7r5 cells are embryonic rat aortic smooth muscle cells that look fibroblast-like and take up foreign genes.

A7r5 cells come from rat aortic smooth muscle. "Aortic" means the aorta, the big artery leaving the heart. Smooth muscle is the involuntary muscle that lines blood vessels and squeezes them, so these are vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs).

The slides list four facts to memorize. They're derived from rat aortic smooth muscle, they have fibroblast-like morphology (flat and spindly, like connective-tissue cells), they're embryonic, and they're suitable for transfection, which means you can introduce foreign (exogenous) genes into them.

{{slide:L1-16|A7r5 cells at low and high density. Notice the long, spindly fibroblast-like shape.}}

:::trap
A7r5 cells are smooth muscle, not skeletal or cardiac muscle. And they're rat, not human. If a question says "these human skeletal muscle cells," something is wrong.
:::

### Subculturing (passaging)

:::key
- Define subculturing and why it is needed
- List the two jobs you did (count, seed)
*Skim:* The exact dish sizes aren't worth memorizing.
:::

The short version: Passaging keeps a cell line alive: count the cells, then seed a known number into a fresh dish.

Subculturing, also called passaging, means taking cells from a full dish, counting them, and re-plating (seeding) a known number into a new dish so the line keeps growing. If you never passage, cells run out of space and nutrients.

The teaching team lifted the cells off the dish for you. Your job was to count living cells with a hemocytometer and a microscope, then seed one dish each.

### Counting with a hemocytometer

:::key
- State the volume of a square and a quadrant
- Apply the North-West counting rule
:::

The short version: A hemocytometer counts cells in a known volume, so one square tells you the concentration.

A hemocytometer is a glass slide with a grid of known size and a chamber of known depth. Because you know the volume above each square, counting the cells in a square tells you how many cells are in that volume.

The course numbers are what you need on the quiz. Each square holds 6.25 nanolitres (nL). On our microscopes you see 12 squares per "quadrant." So one quadrant holds 12 × 6.25 = 75 nL.

{{slide:L1-20|The counting grid and the North-West rule. Count cells touching the top and left lines; skip cells touching the bottom and right lines.}}

The North-West rule stops you double-counting cells that sit on grid lines. Cells on the top and left lines are "in." Cells on the bottom and right lines are "out."

:::hook
Count the square like you read a page. You start at the top-left corner, so the top and left lines are yours. You run off the bottom and right edges, so those lines belong to the next square.
:::

### How to figure it out: the cell math

:::key
- Run the three-step calculation end to end
- Get the nL to mL conversion right
- Sanity-check the final volume
:::

The short version: Average the quadrant counts, convert to cells per mL, then divide what you need by what you have.

Every hemocytometer question uses the same three steps.

{{fig:hemo|The three-step hemocytometer calculation. Average, convert to cells per mL, then divide what you need by what you have.}}

Step 1 is to average your quadrant counts. Step 2 is to turn that into a concentration. Divide the average count by 75 nL to get cells per nL, then multiply by 1,000,000 because there are 10⁶ nL in one mL. Step 3 is to find the volume: volume = cells you want ÷ concentration.

Shortcut: one cell in a 75 nL quadrant equals 13,333 cells/mL. So cells/mL = average count × 13,333.

Worked example from the Week 1 slide: you count 75 cells in one quadrant. That's 75 cells ÷ 75 nL = 1 cell/nL. Multiply by 10⁶ and you get 1,000,000 cells/mL (poll option 4).

Practice example (same type as the practice sheet): counts of 87, 91, 83 and 85 average to 86.5 cells per quadrant. 86.5 ÷ 75 nL = 1.153 cells/nL, which is 1.153 × 10⁶ cells/mL. To seed 400,000 cells, you need 400,000 ÷ 1,153,333 cells/mL = 0.347 mL, or about 347 µL.

:::trap
The two classic slips are forgetting to average (using one count) and getting the nL to mL conversion wrong. One mL is 1,000 µL and 1,000,000 nL. Sanity check your answer: a few hundred µL into a 35 mm dish is reasonable; 0.3 µL or 3,000 mL is not.
:::

:::she
The poll slide asks: "If you counted 75 cells in a quadrant, what is your cell density?" The choices were 750, 750,000, 1,000 and 1,000,000. Only 1,000,000 cells/mL survives the 75 nL conversion.
:::

### Sterile technique in the BSC

:::key
- Explain why sterile technique matters
- Say how an infection could change your results
:::

The short version: The biosafety cabinet keeps microbes out, because contamination is an uncontrolled variable that ruins results.

The BSC is the biosafety cabinet, the glass-fronted hood with filtered air flowing over your work. Cell culture is easily ruined by bacteria, fungi or viruses because the medium is a perfect food source for them too.

The slide asks how infection could affect results. A contaminated dish has microbes eating nutrients, changing the pH, and releasing toxins, so your cells grow differently or die. Any difference you measure later could be the infection instead of myocardin. Contamination is an uncontrolled variable.

:::key

The short version: The Zoe is an inverted fluorescence microscope on a tablet with three colour channels.

The Zoe is a tablet joined to an inverted fluorescence microscope. Inverted means the lens sits under the dish, looking up, so you can image cells in their plastic dish. It has brightfield, three fluorescence channels (blue, green, red) and a digital camera, and it doesn't need a darkroom. You save images to a memory stick.

The Zoe's three channels come back in Week 2 (Hoechst in blue, WGA in green) and Week 6 (Hoechst blue, GFP green, phalloidin red).

### How to figure it out: hemocytometer count to seeding {#howto-l1}

Work it in this order:

1. Lift and resuspend cells
2. Load hemocytometer (75 nL per quadrant)
3. Count 4 quadrants (North-West rule)
4. Average the counts
5. Cells/mL = avg / 75 nL x 1,000,000
6. Volume = cells wanted / concentration
7. Pipette that volume into the new dish

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Not averaging; nL to mL slip; Counting both-edge cells twice.
:::

:::hook
Count, divide by 75, blow up by a million.
:::
