### The big picture in one paragraph

A spin column grabs all the RNA on silica, you check its amount (A260/280) and that it is intact (a gel), then reverse transcriptase copies it into cDNA for the PCR to come.

:::hook
Lyse, Bind, Wash, Elute, then copy to cDNA.

### The idea: a snapshot of gene expression

:::key
- Explain what RNA extraction captures

:::

The short version: Grabbing all the RNA at one moment captures which genes the cell was using then.

mRNA is the working copy a cell makes of each gene it's using right now. If you grab all the RNA from a dish at one moment, you capture which genes were switched on at that moment. Week 5 is the first half of that job: extract all the RNA, check how much and how good it is, and copy it into cDNA. Weeks 7 and 8 do the second half: amplify and count specific transcripts.

### RNA isolation with a spin column

:::key
- List the four column steps in order
- Track where the RNA is at each step
:::

The short version: Lyse, bind, wash, elute: the RNA sticks to silica and only leaves at the end.

A spin column is a small tube with a silica membrane at the bottom that sits inside a collection tube. Silica binds nucleic acids under the right conditions. Every step is the same move: add liquid to the column, centrifuge it ("spin it down"), and deal with the flow-through (the liquid that passes into the tube below). The order is lyse, bind, wash, elute.

The manual's reagents, by name. You lyse the cells in TRK lysis buffer and scrape and homogenise them, which breaks the cells open and releases the RNA while starting to inactivate RNases. You add 70% ethanol, which makes the RNA stick to the silica. You wash with the kit's wash buffers to rinse off protein and salt, do a dry spin to remove leftover ethanol, then elute in DEPC-treated water. DEPC-treated water is water treated to destroy RNases, so your eluted RNA is not chewed up the moment it comes off the column.

{{fig:column|Where the RNA is at each step. Until the elute step it's stuck to the silica, so the flow-through is waste.}}

Lyse means break open the cells and shear (chop up) the genomic DNA so it doesn't clog the column. Bind means apply the lysate (the broken-cell soup) to the silica. RNA binds through salt bridges, and the ethanol in the buffer pulls water out of the way so binding can happen. Wash uses a similar buffer to rinse off weakly bound junk like proteins and salts. Elute uses a regular buffer with no ethanol, so the RNA lets go of the silica and flows into a clean tube.

{{slide:L4-4|The slide's version of the four steps.}}

:::hook
A spin column is a drive-through car wash. Lyse is unloading the car from the truck. Bind is driving onto the conveyor, where the car stays put. Wash sprays the dirt off while the car stays on the track. Elute is the exit gate, the only time the car leaves.
:::

:::trap
"Know where your RNA is at all times." During bind and wash, the RNA is on the column, so you throw out the flow-through. During elute, the RNA is in the flow-through, so you keep it. Throwing away the final flow-through throws away your sample.
:::

### What RNA you get

:::key
- State four ways RNA differs from DNA
- Say which RNA is most abundant and which you want
:::

The short version: You isolate all RNA; rRNA is most abundant but mRNA is what you care about.

RNA differs from DNA in four ways the slide lists: it's single-stranded, it folds into complex 3D shapes, it uses uracil (U) where DNA uses thymine (T), and its sugar has a 2'-OH group that DNA lacks (that extra OH makes RNA less stable).

You isolate all the cell's RNA: rRNA (ribosomal RNA), tRNA (transfer RNA), mRNA and many others. rRNA is by far the most abundant, about 80% of total RNA. The one you actually care about is mRNA, specifically the transcripts of genes myocardin affects, and it's only a few percent of the total.

### Checking quantity and contamination with A260/A280

:::key
- Explain what A260/A280 measures
- Recall the clean-RNA value
:::

The short version: The A260/A280 ratio checks purity; clean RNA sits near 2.0.

A spectrophotometer (the NanoDrop) shines UV light through a tiny drop and measures absorbance. Nucleic acids absorb most strongly at 260 nm, so A260 gives the concentration. Proteins absorb at 280 nm (from their aromatic amino acids). The ratio A260/A280 tells you how pure the sample is. The manual calls roughly 1.8 to 2.0 acceptable for RNA. A ratio well below that means protein (or phenol) contamination.

### Why you measure RNA before reverse transcription

:::key
- Explain why equal input matters
:::

The short version: Equal RNA into every RT reaction keeps the later comparison fair.

You need the concentration so you can put the same amount of RNA into every reverse transcription reaction. If DM cells contributed 500 ng and pMyo cells 1,000 ng, pMyo would look like it had more of every transcript just because more RNA went in. Equal input keeps the comparison fair, and it keeps you in the amount range the RT kit is designed for.

### Checking integrity on a gel

:::key
- Name the bands on a total RNA gel
- Explain the rRNA-as-proxy logic
- Say what a denaturing gel and ladder are for
:::

The short version: You can't see mRNA on a gel, so you judge its integrity from sharp rRNA bands.

Integrity means whether the RNA is whole or chopped up (degraded). RNA is fragile, and enzymes called RNases are everywhere, including on your skin.

{{slide:L4-6|A perfect total RNA gel. Two sharp rRNA bands, the upper one about twice as bright, a faint small band at the bottom, and no smear.}}

The manual runs a 0.6% agarose gel (a loose gel, because rRNA is large) at a relatively low voltage (so the bands stay sharp and don't overheat). It is a non-denaturing gel, meaning the RNA keeps its folded shape, so shape as well as size affects how far it runs.

On the gel you expect three bands: 28S rRNA (top, brightest), 18S rRNA (middle, about half as bright as 28S), and a faint low band of small RNA (5S rRNA and tRNA). A 28S:18S ratio of about 2 with no background smear means intact RNA. A smear down the lane means the RNA has been degraded.

You can't see your mRNAs on the gel because there are thousands of different ones at different sizes, so they blend into a faint background. Instead, you infer mRNA integrity from rRNA integrity. If the rRNA bands are sharp, nothing in the tube was chewing up RNA, so the mRNA should be intact too. A gel also shows genomic DNA contamination as a high band near the well.

A denaturing gel contains a chemical that unfolds the RNA's 3D shapes. Without it, folded RNA moves through the gel based on shape as well as size, and bands look odd. You run a ladder (fragments of known size) so you can identify which band is which.

:::hook
rRNA is the canary in the coal mine. You can't see the mRNA, but if the canary (the big rRNA bands) is healthy, nothing toxic (RNase) was in the tube.
:::

### Reverse transcription: RNA to cDNA

:::key
- Explain why RT is needed
- Compare poly-T and random primers
:::

The short version: Reverse transcriptase copies RNA into cDNA because PCR enzymes only copy DNA.

PCR enzymes only copy DNA, so you first copy the RNA into cDNA (complementary DNA) with reverse transcriptase. The slide calls this RT-PCR.

{{slide:L4-8|Reverse transcription: primers anneal at about 25 °C, then the enzyme extends at 46 °C to make a DNA copy.}}

The manual runs three temperature stages. 25 °C for 5 minutes lets the primers anneal (stick) to the RNA. 46 °C for 20 minutes is the actual reverse transcription, where the enzyme extends the primers into cDNA. 95 °C for 1 minute inactivates the enzyme, stopping the reaction. (One short heads-up on wording: another part of the course heat-snaps RNA, warming it to about 70 °C and cooling it fast to melt out folded secondary structure before a gel. That is a different step from RT; altered migration after heat-snapping is lost structure, not degradation.)

The slide asks what sequence you use as a primer. Poly-T (oligo-dT) primers bind the poly-A tail, the string of A's on the end of mRNAs, so they select for mRNA. Random primers bind everywhere, which helps capture long mRNAs all the way along, but they also copy tRNA and rRNA.

:::trap
Why not measure DNA concentration by spectrophotometry after RT? Because the tube still contains everything else that absorbs at 260 nm: the leftover RNA template, unused primers and free nucleotides. A260 would count all of it as "DNA," so the number would be meaningless. ddPCR is how you measure the specific cDNA you care about.
:::

:::conn
The cDNA you made in Week 5 is the template you load into ddPCR in Week 7 ("cDNA from week 5" is written on the setup slide).
:::

### How to figure it out: spin column and rt {#howto-l4}

Work it in this order:

1. Lyse cells, shear DNA
2. Bind RNA to silica (ethanol)
3. Wash off junk
4. Elute RNA in plain buffer
5. Check A260/280 and run a gel
6. Reverse transcribe RNA to cDNA

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Tossing the final flow-through; Measuring DNA by A260 after RT.
:::
