## Week 8: reading ddPCR data {#l7}

:::story
The final week is pure analysis: read the droplet plots, set thresholds, spot the failure where every droplet is positive, and choose a housekeeping gene from your own data.
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Check for two clean bands, place the threshold in the gap, and watch the controls. All-positive is a failure (no negatives to count), not a triumph. Normalize to whichever housekeeping gene stays flattest.

:::hook
Two bands good, all-blue bad.
:::

### How to figure it out: reading a ddPCR plot

:::key
- Run the five-point plot check
- Separate what data show from what they mean
:::

The short version: Check for two bands, the rain between them, the threshold, a negative band, and the control.

Every ddPCR interpretation question can be answered with the same five checks. The slides keep asking two separate things: what the data show (what you can see in the plot) and what the data mean (what it tells you about the cells).

1. Are there two clear bands? A clean high band and a clean low band means good separation.
2. How many dots sit between the bands? Those in-between droplets are called "rain." Lots of rain makes the threshold hard to place.
3. Where's the threshold line, and does it sit in the gap between the bands?
4. Is there at least one negative band? Without negatives, Poisson math can't work.
5. What's the control doing? The no-primer well should show only negatives.

### The hyacinth, daffodil and C1 example

:::key
- Interpret the three-well example
- Explain why you can't rank genes from the dot plot alone
:::

The short version: C1 with no primers shows only negatives, which is the baseline for no product.

{{slide:L7-9|Three wells of cDNA from A7r5 cells in growth medium (GM). A1 has hyacinth primers, B1 daffodil primers, C1 no primers.}}

Hyacinth and daffodil are code names for real genes. In A1 there are positives up around 9,000 to 10,000, negatives near 1,500, and a lot of rain between them. B1 has positives around 8,000 and negatives near 1,500, with tighter bands. C1 has only a low band.

{{slide:L7-10|Reaction C1 on its own: every droplet is negative.}}

For C1, the data show only negative droplets. The data mean nothing amplified without primers, so there's no contamination or primer-free signal, and the background fluorescence of a negative droplet sits near 1,000. C1 is the negative control that tells you what "no product" looks like.

The slide asks which gene was more expressed in these cells, hyacinth or daffodil. The safest answer is "need more information." The dot plot doesn't show the copy numbers, the two wells may not have had identical cDNA input, and to compare genes in a real sense you'd want concentrations from the software and a housekeeping gene for context.

:::she
Slide 12 asks: why are positives in A1 higher in fluorescence intensity than B1? The amplitude of a positive droplet depends on how much dsDNA dye is bound at the end, not on how many copies it started with. Different primer sets make products of different lengths and amplify with different efficiency. A longer product binds more dye, so its positive droplets glow brighter.
:::

### Thresholds and rain

:::key
- Place a threshold correctly
- Explain what rain is and how to reduce it
:::

The short version: Put the threshold in the empty gap; rain is late or weak amplification you can optimize away.

{{slide:L7-13|The software draws a threshold between blue (positive) and gray (negative). You'll adjust it yourself.}}

The software picks a threshold fluorescence above which droplets count as positive. You can move it manually. A threshold should sit in the empty gap between the two bands. In A1, there are so many in-between droplets that wherever you place the line, some rain gets miscounted.

Rain comes from droplets where amplification started late or ran inefficiently. To improve a replicate, optimize the reaction: try a different annealing temperature (a temperature gradient run), check primer design, or dilute the template if it's crowded. The goal is two tight bands with a clean gap.

### One band only: the tulip well

:::key
- Interpret a one-band plot against the control
- Explain why all-positive can't be quantified
:::

The short version: A single high band with no negatives is saturated, so Poisson breaks and you must dilute.

{{slide:L7-14|Well A1 with tulip primers. Every droplet sits in one band near 10,000.}}

When there's only one band, you can't tell from that well alone whether it's all positives or all negatives. Compare it to a no-primer control: if the control's negatives sit near 1,000 and this band sits near 10,000, every droplet is positive.

All positive means the reaction is saturated. Nearly every droplet got at least one target, so there are no negatives. Poisson needs the fraction of negatives (λ = −ln(fraction negative)), and if that fraction is zero, the math breaks: there could be 3 copies per droplet or 300, and you can't know. The fix is to dilute the cDNA and run again.

:::trap
Practice question 5c. "All droplets blue" sounds like a great result, and it isn't. ddPCR counts by comparing positives to negatives. No negatives means no count. Other causes of all-blue are a threshold placed inside the negative band, or contamination or nonspecific product (like primer dimers) lighting up every droplet. The no-primer control helps you tell these apart.
:::

### Which primer set was in which well?

:::key
- Explain how to identify a primer set from its plot
:::

The short version: Each primer set gives a characteristic positive amplitude, so you can match mixed-up wells.

The practice sheet shows three optimization wells (A01, B01, C01) at 284, 221 and 219 copies/µL, each with positives at a different height. If the primer labels got mixed up, use the amplitude. Each primer set gives a characteristic positive amplitude, because its product length and efficiency are fixed. Compare each well's positive band height with a run where you know which primers were used. You could also run the ddPCR products on an agarose gel and match band sizes to each primer set's expected product length.

### Choosing a housekeeping gene

:::key
- State how to choose a housekeeping gene
:::

The short version: Pick the housekeeping gene that stays flattest across conditions and gives clean data.

{{slide:L7-15|Each team had one column (one condition) and each primer set had its own row.}}

Your plate had one column per group (conditions DM, pMYOCD and pGFP) and one row per primer set. Housekeeping options were β-actin, GAPDH and rTbp1; contractile genes were CNN, MHC, SMA and SM22α; and there was a no-primer control.

Pick the housekeeping gene whose copies change least across DM, pMYOCD and pGFP. It should also give clean ddPCR data (good band separation, enough positives and negatives). If a "housekeeping" gene moves with treatment, normalizing to it adds error instead of removing it.

### Improving the draft graph

:::key
- List concrete fixes for the draft graph
- Connect the fixes to the writing standards
:::

The short version: Drop the gene-over-itself bars, use real names, and group conditions side by side.

{{slide:L7-16|A draft bar graph of every gene normalized to housekeeping gene 1, grouped by condition.}}

The slide asks how to improve this graph. A few clear fixes: drop the housekeeping gene 1 bars, since a gene divided by itself is always 1.0 and tells you nothing. Use the real gene names instead of "ContractileGene1." Group bars by gene with the three conditions side by side, so the comparison you care about (DM vs pMyo vs pEGFP for each gene) sits next to each other. Give each condition its own colour and a legend. Show replicates with error bars. Consider expressing pMyo relative to the pEGFP control (fold change), since pEGFP is the right comparison for myocardin's effect.

:::easy
Error bars: SD or SEM? The mean is the average. Variance and its square root, the standard deviation (SD), measure how spread out your replicates are. The standard error of the mean (SEM) measures how precise your estimate of the mean is, and it is SEM = SD / √n, where n is the number of replicates. So SEM is always smaller than SD, and it shrinks as you add replicates. Use SD when you want to show the spread of the data, and SEM when you want to show how well you have pinned down the average. Easy mark: say which one your error bars are, because the same data looks tighter with SEM than with SD.
:::

:::conn
Week 2's writing primer applies here. A figure is part of your writing, so it has to be clear, accurate, relevant, precise, concise and appropriately detailed. Bars that always equal 1.0 aren't relevant; vague labels aren't clear.
:::

### How to figure it out: reading a droplet plot {#howto-l7}

Work it in this order:

1. Are there two clear bands?
2. How much rain between them?
3. Is the threshold in the gap?
4. Is there a negative band?
5. What is the no-primer control doing?
6. Then quantify and normalize

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Calling all-blue a success; Threshold inside the negative band.
:::

Show is the eyes; mean is the brain.
