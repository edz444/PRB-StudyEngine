## Week 2: fluorescence microscopy {#l2}

:::story
Now you can grow cells. This week is about seeing them: how fluorescent dyes light up specific parts, and how to pick the right microscope channel.
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

A fluorophore soaks up light at one wavelength and spits it out at a longer one. Match a dye's excitation and emission to a Zoe channel, and you can light up DNA, membrane or actin one colour at a time.

:::hook
In short and blue, out long and red.
:::

### What fluorescence is

:::key
- Define excitation and emission
- Explain why emission is always a longer wavelength
:::

The short version: A fluorophore absorbs short-wavelength light and emits longer-wavelength light, losing a little energy as heat.

A fluorophore is a molecule that absorbs light of one wavelength and then gives off light of a different, longer wavelength. Wavelength (λ, "lambda") is what sets the colour of light: short wavelengths are violet and blue, longer ones are green, then red.

Absorbing is called excitation. Giving off light is called emission. The emitted light always has a longer wavelength than the light that excited it, because the molecule loses a little energy as heat in between. That gap between the two peaks is the Stokes shift.

{{slide:L2-13|Light goes in at one colour, a bit of energy is lost, and light comes out at a longer, lower-energy colour.}}

:::hook
Fluorescence is a toll road. You drive in with a full tank of energy (short, blue light), pay a toll inside the molecule, and leave with less (longer, redder light). You never come out with more than you went in with.
:::

### Reading an excitation-emission spectrum

:::key
- Tell excitation from emission on a plot
- Justify your answer with the wavelength rule
:::

The short version: On a spectrum, the left peak is excitation and the right peak is emission.

{{slide:L2-14|Peak #1 sits at shorter wavelength, so it's the excitation spectrum. Peak #2 is shifted to the right, so it's the emission.}}

The excitation curve is the one on the left (shorter wavelength) and the emission curve is on the right (longer wavelength). The answer to the slide's poll is A: excitation #1, emission #2. You know because emission is always at a longer wavelength than excitation.

### Choosing a channel on the Zoe

:::key
- Match a dye to the right Zoe channel
- Spot the impossible excite-high collect-low option
:::

The short version: Pick the channel whose excitation and emission filters match the dye's peaks.

To image a fluorophore, you pick the channel whose excitation filter matches the fluorophore's excitation peak and whose emission filter matches its emission peak.

{{slide:L2-15|The Zoe's filters. Blue channel excites at 355/40 nm and collects 433/36 nm, which matches a DNA dye that absorbs near 350 and emits near 460.}}

For the DNA-binding dye on that slide (excites near 350 nm, emits near 460 nm), you excite at 355 nm, collect at 433 nm, and use the blue channel. That's poll option 4.

| Zoe channel | Excitation filter | Emission filter |
|---|---|---|
| Blue | 355/40 nm | 433/36 nm |
| Green | 480/17 nm | 517/23 nm |
| Red | 556/20 nm | 615/61 nm |

The number after the slash is the filter's width. 355/40 means it lets through light from about 335 to 375 nm.

:::trap
Two poll options swap excitation and emission (excite at 480, collect at 355). That can't work: you can never collect at a shorter wavelength than you excite. If the "collect" number is smaller than the "excite" number, cross it out.
:::

### Our fluorophores

:::key
- Match each dye to its target and colour
- Recall the rough excitation and emission of Hoechst and WGA
:::

The short version: Hoechst marks DNA in blue, WGA marks the membrane in green, phalloidin marks actin in red.

Fluorophores are useful in biology because they can bind, or be engineered to bind, specific molecules. That lets you light up one cell part at a time.

{{slide:L2-17|The two Week 2 stains. Hoechst lights up DNA in blue; wheat germ agglutinin lights up the plasma membrane in green.}}

Hoechst 33342 (shortened to HOE) is a nucleic acid stain that binds double-stranded DNA. Its excitation max is about 350 nm and its emission max about 461 nm, so it shows in the blue channel and marks the nucleus.

Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) is a plant protein that selectively binds carbohydrates. The outer surface of the plasma membrane is coated in sugar chains, so fluorescent WGA outlines the cell edge. Excitation max is about 495 nm and emission max about 519 nm, so it's in the green channel.

Phalloidin (introduced on this slide, used in Week 6) binds F-actin, the filament form of actin. It's usually linked to a red Alexa fluorophore.

{{slide:L2-16|DNA dyes sit in the DNA helix; phalloidin grabs actin filaments and carries a fluorophore with it.}}

:::hook
Blue is the brain, green is the skin, red is the skeleton. Hoechst marks the nucleus (control centre), WGA marks the membrane (outer skin), phalloidin marks actin (the scaffold).
:::

### Image analysis and doubling time

:::key
- Explain how doubling time is measured
- Avoid mixing a per-field count with a whole-dish count
*Skim:* The exact ImageJ button sequence isn't quiz material.
:::

The short version: Count cells at Day 0 and Day 7 to work out how long the population takes to double.

In lab you counted cells two ways: by eye and with ImageJ (regions of interest and the cell counter tool). One goal was to follow the cells from seeding (Day 0) to Day 7 and work out their doubling time.

{{slide:L2-19|Day 0 cells were counted as seeded; Day 7 cells were counted from HOE/WGA images in ImageJ.}}

Day 0 cells aren't stuck down yet; they're still floating in suspension. By Day 7 they've adhered (attached) to the dish and are dividing.

Doubling time is how long the population takes to double. The formula is

doubling time = t × ln(2) ÷ ln(N_final ÷ N_start)

where t is the time between counts. A made-up example: you seed 50,000 cells and count 800,000 on Day 7. The ratio is 16, which is 2⁴, so the cells doubled 4 times in 7 days. Doubling time = 7 ÷ 4 = 1.75 days, or 42 hours.

:::trap
A Day 7 count from one field of view isn't the whole dish. To get total cells you scale your field count up by (dish area ÷ field area), or work with density in both counts. Mixing a whole-dish count with a per-field count gives nonsense doubling times.
:::

### The writing primer

:::key
- Recall the six writing standards
- Know what references vs acknowledgements mean
:::

The short version: Every piece of writing is graded on being clear, accurate, relevant, precise, concise and appropriately detailed.

Every piece of writing in this course is graded on six standards. Effective writing is clear, accurate, relevant, precise, concise and appropriately detailed.

Clear means a logical flow, and each statement can only be read one way. Graders can't assume you meant the right interpretation, so give evidence of your understanding. Accurate means correct; clear but wrong still fails. Relevant means nothing that distracts from the point. Precise can be numerical (sensible decimal places) or linguistic (the right word). Concise means direct: if you can remove a word, sentence or paragraph without changing the meaning, remove it. Appropriately detailed means enough detail for your audience, which requires knowing the subject well enough to tell what matters.

:::hook
CAR-P-CAD. Design your writing like a car on CAD software: Clear, Accurate, Relevant, Precise, Concise, Appropriately Detailed.
:::

For Lab Assignment 1, results and discussion should always point to the figure or table that supports each claim. References are formal sources you consulted. Acknowledgements are people or sources that helped you understand or explain.

### How to figure it out: from dye to the right channel {#howto-l2}

Work it in this order:

1. Dye binds its target
2. Excite at the dye's absorption peak
3. Dye emits at a longer wavelength
4. Pick the channel whose filters match
5. Collect the monochrome image
6. Assign a display colour

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Collecting at a shorter wavelength than you excite; Wrong channel for the dye.
:::

:::hook
Left peak goes in, right peak comes out.
:::
