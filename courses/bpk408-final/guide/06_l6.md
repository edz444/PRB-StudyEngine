## Week 7: droplet digital PCR {#l6}

:::story
This week counts the molecular result. Ordinary PCR can't tell you how much you started with, so ddPCR splits the reaction into thousands of droplets and counts them.
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Each droplet gets zero or one target cDNA. After PCR, droplets with target glow and droplets without stay dim. Count positives vs negatives to get an absolute number, then normalize to a housekeeping gene.

:::hook
Split it, run it, count the lit cups.
:::

### The plan

:::key
- State what ddPCR counts and why
:::

The short version: ddPCR counts the cDNA molecules for one gene, which equals the mRNA that was present.

Reverse transcription made one cDNA copy for every mRNA present. ddPCR counts the cDNA molecules for one gene at a time. Count the cDNA and you know how many mRNA molecules of that gene were in your sample.

{{slide:L6-7|mRNA becomes cDNA (RT), then ddPCR counts cDNA molecules of one gene.}}

### PCR is exponential, so it isn't quantitative

:::key
- Explain why end-point PCR can't be quantitative
:::

The short version: PCR plateaus at the end, so tubes with different starts finish the same.

PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies a chosen stretch of DNA using two primers that flank it. Each cycle roughly doubles the number of copies, so after n cycles one molecule becomes 2ⁿ. After 30 cycles that's 2³⁰ = 1,073,741,824 molecules from one template.

{{slide:L6-9|The PCR curve: flat background, then the exponential phase, then a plateau when reagents run out.}}

Ordinary PCR isn't quantitative. By the end of the reaction it has stopped being exponential and hit a plateau, because primers and nucleotides run out and the enzyme tires. A tube that started with 10 copies and one that started with 1,000 can end at the same plateau. So the final product can't tell you how many templates you started with.

:::hook
End-point PCR is judging how many people entered a stadium by looking at it when it's full. Every full stadium looks the same, whether people started arriving at noon or at 6 pm.
:::

### Three generations of PCR

:::key
- Compare the three PCR generations
:::

The short version: Standard PCR says yes/no, qPCR estimates from timing, ddPCR counts molecules.

{{slide:L6-11|First generation: PCR on a gel (qualitative). Second: real-time qPCR (relative). Third: droplet digital PCR (absolute counts).}}

Standard PCR detects product only after all cycles, so it tells you yes or no. qPCR (quantitative or real-time PCR) detects product at each cycle and extrapolates the starting amount from when the curve rises; earlier rise means more template. ddPCR doesn't estimate from timing at all. It counts target molecules.

### How ddPCR works

:::key
- Describe the four ddPCR steps
- Explain why a positive droplet glows
:::

The short version: Split the reaction into droplets so each gets zero or one target, then count the ones that glow.

{{fig:droplets|One positive droplet and one negative droplet, at the start and end of the run. This is practice question 5a.}}

First, set up a PCR mix with known amounts of template (cDNA), primers for one gene, nucleotides, polymerase, a dsDNA-binding dye and buffer. Second, the droplet generator mixes that with oil and splits it into about 20,000 tiny droplets. Each droplet is its own little PCR reaction. The cDNA spreads out at random so that most droplets get either zero copies of the target or one. Third, run PCR on all the droplets at once in a thermocycler. Fourth, the droplet reader measures each droplet's fluorescence one by one and counts them.

{{slide:L6-13|Splitting the reaction. Some droplets get a target cDNA (YES), most don't (NO), and the YES:NO ratio rises with target concentration.}}

In a droplet that got a target molecule, PCR runs to plateau and makes huge amounts of double-stranded product. The dye binds double-stranded DNA and fluoresces, so the droplet glows (green on the slides, blue in the software plot). In a droplet with no target, there's nothing for the primers to copy, almost no dsDNA forms, and the droplet stays dim (gray in the software).

:::hook
ddPCR is an egg carton for molecules. Spill a few marbles (target cDNA) into thousands of cups. After PCR, every cup with a marble lights up like a brake light. You don't need to know how bright; you just count lit cups.
:::

{{slide:L6-17|The four-step workflow: set up, make droplets with oil, run PCR on droplets, read and count.}}

### Reading the plot

:::key
- Read positives and negatives off a droplet plot
- Explain why ddPCR is absolute, not brightness-based
:::

The short version: Positives form a high band counted as 1, negatives a low band counted as 0.

{{slide:L6-18|Each dot is one droplet. Positives (top band) count as 1, negatives (bottom band) count as 0.}}

The software plots every droplet as a dot: event number (which droplet) on the x-axis, fluorescence amplitude on the y-axis. Positive droplets form a high band and count as 1. Negative droplets form a low band and count as 0. From the ratio of positives to negatives, the software uses "cool math" to report copies per µL of reaction, which you can convert to copies per input cDNA, or copies of transcript per cell.

The cool math is Poisson statistics. Some droplets get two or three targets by chance, and they still light up as one positive, so just counting positives undercounts the real number of molecules. Poisson corrects for that using the fraction of droplets that are negative: average copies per droplet is λ = −ln(N_negative / N_total), where N_negative is the number of negative droplets and N_total is all droplets. Worked example: if 15,000 of 20,000 droplets are negative, the fraction negative is 0.75, so λ = −ln(0.75) = 0.29 copies per droplet on average. You won't usually calculate it by hand, but it explains one exam trap below.

:::trap
Why is ddPCR "absolute" even though each droplet just reads yes or no? Because brightness doesn't carry the number; the count of yes droplets does. A droplet with one starting copy and one with three end up about equally bright at plateau.
:::

### What could make one condition have more copies?

:::key
- Give several reasons one reaction has more copies
- Separate biological from technical causes
:::

The short version: Real biology is one reason, but more cells, more RNA or better RT also raise every gene.

{{slide:L6-23|α-SMA reads 100 copies in DM, 200 in pMyo and 150 in pEGFP. Why might these differ?}}

The slide asks for three or four reasons a reaction could start with more molecules of a gene. Real biology is one: myocardin really did raise α-SMA. But several technical reasons do it too. More cells were in that dish (different seeding, growth, or cell death from transfection). More RNA went into the RT reaction, or the RNA was less degraded. RT worked more efficiently in that tube. More cDNA was pipetted into the ddPCR reaction. Any of those raise every gene, not just α-SMA.

### Normalizing to a housekeeping gene

:::key
- Explain what normalizing does
- Name the housekeeping and contractile genes
- See why beta-actin is risky here
:::

The short version: Dividing target by a steady housekeeping gene cancels the technical differences.

A housekeeping gene is one the cell expresses at a steady level no matter what you do to it, because it runs basic cell functions. Dividing your target gene's copies by a housekeeping gene's copies from the same sample cancels the technical differences above, because those raise both genes equally. Then you can make fair pairwise comparisons between conditions.

normalized expression = copies of target gene ÷ copies of housekeeping gene (same sample)

Our housekeeping genes are GAPDH, rTbp1 and β-actin1. The contractile genes are CNN, MHC, α-SMA and SM22α.

Know what each gene is for, because she can ask you to sort them. GAPDH is a classic reference (housekeeping) gene. MHC (smooth muscle myosin heavy chain), CNN (calponin), α-SMA (alpha smooth muscle actin) and SM22α (also called transgelin) are contractile or smooth-muscle-marker genes, the ones myocardin should raise. β-actin is a reference gene on paper but is itself an actin, so it is a risky reference in an actin experiment.

:::trap
Flag for you to check against your own manual: the slide primer list I built from shows rTbp1 (rat TATA-box binding protein, a housekeeping gene) as the second reference gene. A manual breakdown you pasted instead lists Integrin beta-1 (ITβ1), which is an adhesion gene, not a housekeeping gene. These are very different, so confirm which one your manual actually uses before the quiz. If it is ITβ1, treat it as an adhesion marker, not a reference gene, and normalise to GAPDH.
:::

A made-up example using the slide's α-SMA numbers. Say GAPDH read 1,000 copies in DM, 1,250 in pMyo and 1,500 in pEGFP. Normalized α-SMA is 100 ÷ 1,000 = 0.10 (DM), 200 ÷ 1,250 = 0.16 (pMyo) and 150 ÷ 1,500 = 0.10 (pEGFP). The raw pEGFP bump disappears (it was just more starting material), while pMyo is still 1.6 times higher than pEGFP.

{{slide:L6-26|Two cDNA libraries. HG (housekeeping) is about the same in both; C1 and C2 (contractile genes) differ. Comparing C1 to HG inside each tube is the fair comparison.}}

:::hook
Normalizing is comparing two dealerships fairly. One sold 200 cars and one sold 150, but the first had twice the customers walk in. Divide sales by walk-ins (the housekeeping gene) before you decide who sold better.
:::

:::trap
β-actin is on the housekeeping list, but this is an actin experiment. If myocardin shifts how cells make actin, β-actin may not stay steady in pMyo cells. A good housekeeping gene is one whose copies stay about the same across DM, pMyo and pEGFP in your own data, which is how Week 8 asks you to choose.
:::

### How to figure it out: ddpcr, start to count {#howto-l6}

Work it in this order:

1. Mix cDNA, primers, dye, enzyme
2. Generator makes droplets with oil
3. Each droplet gets 0 or 1 target
4. Run PCR on all droplets
5. Positives glow, negatives stay dim
6. Reader counts positive vs negative
7. Poisson math gives copies
8. Normalize to housekeeping gene

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Reading brightness as copy number; Normalizing to a gene that moves with treatment.
:::
