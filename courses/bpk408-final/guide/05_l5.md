## Week 6: staining and imaging {#l5}

:::story
Here you read the structural result of myocardin: stain the cells, image three colours, and measure how many got transfected and whether their actin reorganized.
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Fix, permeabilize, then stain DNA blue and actin red; GFP is already green. Keep exposure identical across conditions, count green over blue for transfection efficiency, and judge the actin shape.

:::hook
Blue brain, green winners, red skeleton.
:::

### What each colour shows, and why

:::key
- Match each channel to its molecule
- Explain that images are really grey data
:::

The short version: Blue is DNA, green is GFP, red is actin; colour is assigned to monochrome images afterward.

You image three channels plus brightfield (BF). Blue is Hoechst, marking DNA in the nucleus, which lets you count every cell. Green is GFP, which is natural fluorescence from the plasmid and marks transfected cells. Red is phalloidin, marking F-actin, which shows the shape of the cytoskeleton.

{{slide:L5-5|Brightfield next to the merged fluorescence image.}}

The slide makes a point worth knowing for the quiz. Technically you collect three monochrome (black-and-white) images at different wavelengths. Colour is something you assign afterward. You can show them in any colours you like, as long as you say which is which. The next slide adds that colour-blind-friendly choices like magenta, yellow and cyan are better than red and green, since about 4.5% of people can't tell red from green.

### Co-localization

:::key
- Reason about which signals co-localize from where each molecule sits
*Skim:* The instructor's exact poll answer wasn't on the slides.
:::

The short version: GFP fills the cell so it overlaps both others; Hoechst and actin barely overlap.

Co-localization means two signals appear in the same place in the image. The slide polls which pairs you'd expect to overlap.

Reason it out from where each molecule lives. Hoechst is only in the nucleus. Phalloidin-stained F-actin is in the cytoplasm, mostly as filaments. GFP is a small soluble protein that fills the cytoplasm and also slips into the nucleus. So GFP overlaps partly with both of the others, while Hoechst and actin barely overlap at all.

### The staining protocol

:::key
- Put the staining steps in order
- Explain why permeabilizing comes before phalloidin
- Say why washes prevent background
:::

The short version: Fix, wash, permeabilize, then stain, because phalloidin can't cross an intact membrane.

{{fig:stain|The staining order, and why each step comes where it does.}}

Fix first. A fixative cross-links proteins so the cell is frozen in place and stops changing (it also kills the cells). Wash. Then permeabilize, which uses a detergent to punch holes in the membrane. Phalloidin can't cross an intact membrane, so without permeabilizing, it never reaches the actin. Then stain with Hoechst, wash, stain with phalloidin, and wash again.

Washes remove unbound dye. Unbound dye floating around or stuck to the dish glows too, so skipped or rushed washes give high background.

:::hook
Fix, wash, perm: "Freeze the scene, wipe the floor, open the doors." You can't paint the inside of a car (actin) until you've opened the doors (permeabilized).
:::

### Direct staining versus immunostaining

:::key
- Contrast direct and indirect staining
- Explain why GFP needs neither
:::

The short version: Direct stains bind the target themselves; immunostaining uses an antibody relay you aren't doing.

{{slide:L5-15|Our three labels. Hoechst and phalloidin are direct stains; GFP is natural fluorescence.}}

Direct staining means the dye itself binds the target. Hoechst binds DNA directly, and phalloidin binds F-actin directly while carrying its fluorophore.

{{slide:L5-16|The alternative we're not doing. In immunostaining, an antibody finds the target and a second fluorescent antibody finds the first.}}

Immunostaining (also called immunocytochemistry or antibody labelling) is indirect. A primary antibody binds the target protein, and a fluorescent secondary antibody binds the primary. It's more flexible (you can label almost any protein), but it has more steps, more washes, and more places for background to creep in. GFP needs neither, since the protein glows on its own.

:::trap
GFP is not a stain. Nobody adds it at the staining step. It's made by the cell from the plasmid, so it's labelled "natural fluorescence."
:::

### Collecting images you can measure

:::key
- Explain saturation and why it loses information
- Say why underexposing is safer
:::

The short version: A saturated pixel maxes at 255 and loses data, so slightly underexpose instead.

{{slide:L5-17|Three exposures of the same field. A is too dim, C is saturated. B is the one to save.}}

An image is data: each pixel holds a number for how much light hit it. On the Zoe that's 0 (black) to 255 (white). A plot profile graphs pixel values along a line you draw across the image.

{{slide:L5-18|Plot profiles. On the left, values rise and fall below 255, so differences are recorded. On the right, the bright region hits 255 and flattens, so detail is lost.}}

If too much light hits a pixel, it maxes out at 255, which is called saturation or overexposure. Every brighter structure also reads 255, so you can't tell them apart or measure them. That information is gone for good. Slight underexposure is safer because a dim image can be brightened later in ImageJ for presentation without losing anything, as long as it wasn't saturated in the first place.

:::hook
Saturation is a speedometer that tops out at 255. A car doing 260 and a car doing 400 both read 255, and you'll never know which was faster.
:::

Practice question 3a asks for tips to make images comparable across conditions. Use identical exposure, gain and light intensity for every condition in a channel, so brightness differences come from the cells, not the settings. Set exposure on the brightest sample so nothing saturates anywhere. Choose fields without bias, for example by finding them in brightfield or the blue channel instead of hunting for the greenest cells. Take several fields per condition, collect every channel for a field before moving the stage, and save the raw, unadjusted files.

### Why phalloidin background can be high

:::key
- Give two reasons for high red background, each with a cause
:::

The short version: Too little washing, too much dye, dried cells or high gain all add red background.

Practice question 3b describes high red background. Likely causes are not washing enough after phalloidin (unbound dye stays behind), using too much phalloidin or staining too long, letting the cells dry out during the protocol (dried cells bind dye non-specifically), and imaging with the red exposure or gain set too high. Each one adds red signal that isn't sitting on actin filaments.

### Transfection efficiency

:::key
- Write the transfection efficiency formula
- Explain how to decide GFP-positive fairly
:::

The short version: Transfection efficiency is green cells over total nuclei, times 100.

Transfection efficiency is the proportion of cells that took in the plasmid.

transfection efficiency (%) = GFP-positive cells ÷ total cells (Hoechst nuclei) × 100

{{slide:L5-21|Count blue nuclei for the total, count green cells for the transfected, and overlay them to match each green cell to a nucleus.}}

A made-up example: one field has 230 Hoechst nuclei and 46 green cells. 46 ÷ 230 × 100 = 20%.

The slide asks how you'd decide whether a cell is GFP-positive. Set an intensity threshold above background, using a control dish that has no GFP (like DM cells) to see what "not green" looks like, then apply the same threshold to every image. Your eye is not consistent across images; a fixed rule is.

### Reading actin morphology

:::key
- List what to look for in actin shape
- Explain why actin is imaged after myocardin
:::

The short version: Parallel stress fibres running the cell's length suggest a more contractile phenotype.

{{slide:L5-22|Phalloidin-stained A7r5 cells. Look at whether fibres run parallel, hug the edge, or cross over the nucleus.}}

The slide gives three questions to ask of each cell. Are most of the fibres parallel? Is most of the actin concentrated around the periphery (edge)? Are there clear fibres crossing the nucleus? Long, parallel stress fibres running the length of the cell are what you'd expect from a more contractile smooth muscle cell.

Practice question 3c asks why we image actin after myocardin transfection. Myocardin switches on contractile genes, including α-SMA, which is itself an actin. If myocardin pushes A7r5 cells toward a contractile phenotype, the actin cytoskeleton should become more organized, with thicker, parallel stress fibres. Imaging actin gives a structural, protein-level readout that complements the mRNA counts from ddPCR.

:::conn
Week 6 shows whether the cell's structure changed. Weeks 7 and 8 show whether the contractile gene mRNA changed. A strong answer links both to myocardin's job from Weeks 3 and 4.
:::

### How to figure it out: fix, stain, image, measure {#howto-l5}

Work it in this order:

1. Fix the cells
2. Wash
3. Permeabilize
4. Hoechst stain (blue)
5. Wash
6. Phalloidin stain (red)
7. Image 3 channels at fixed settings
8. Count GFP/total and read actin

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Staining actin before permeabilizing; Saturated pixels at 255; Different settings per condition.
:::
