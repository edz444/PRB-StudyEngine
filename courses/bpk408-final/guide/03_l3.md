## Weeks 3-4: myocardin and transfection {#l3}

:::story
This is the heart of the whole project: the gene you force into the cells, and how it gets there. The slides for these weeks weren't uploaded, so this is built to answer the practice questions.

### The big picture in one paragraph

Myocardin is a coactivator that joins SRF on CArG boxes and switches on contractile genes. You deliver it on the plasmid pMYOCD-IRES-GFP, which also makes GFP so you can see which cells took it up.

:::hook
Myocardin Makes Muscle; GFP marks the winners.
:::

### What myocardin does

:::key
- Explain how myocardin turns a gene on
- Name its partner and binding site
- Avoid drawing myocardin on DNA directly
:::

The short version: Myocardin doesn't bind DNA; it joins SRF on a CArG box and switches on smooth muscle genes.

Myocardin is a transcriptional coactivator. A transcription factor is a protein that binds DNA and controls whether a nearby gene is read. A coactivator doesn't bind DNA itself; it piggybacks on a transcription factor and makes it much better at turning the gene on.

Myocardin's partner is SRF (serum response factor). SRF binds a short DNA sequence called a CArG box in the promoter of many smooth muscle genes. The promoter is the stretch of DNA just upstream of a gene where transcription starts.

When SRF sits on the CArG box alone, the contractile gene is read weakly or not at all. When myocardin joins SRF, the pair recruits RNA polymerase II (the enzyme that copies DNA into mRNA), and the gene is transcribed strongly. More mRNA means more contractile protein, and the cell shifts toward a differentiated, contractile smooth muscle phenotype.

{{fig:myocd|Practice question 1 in one picture. The same gene with and without myocardin. SRF is always on the CArG box; myocardin is the switch.}}

The genes myocardin turns on are the contractile genes on your ddPCR primer list: α-SMA (alpha smooth muscle actin), SM22α (also called transgelin), CNN (calponin) and MHC (smooth muscle myosin heavy chain).

:::hook
SRF is a car parked at the start line with the engine idling. Myocardin is the driver who gets in and floors it. The car is always there; whether the gene goes anywhere depends on the driver.
:::

:::trap
Don't draw myocardin binding the DNA directly. It binds SRF, and SRF binds the CArG box. Practice question 1 asks for a "simplified gene structure," so label the promoter, CArG box, SRF, myocardin, the coding region and the mRNA.
:::

### The plasmid: pMYOCD-IRES-GFP

:::key
- Explain what the IRES does
- Say why a green cell is also a myocardin cell
- Know what pEGFP controls for
:::

The short version: One mRNA makes two proteins because an IRES lets a second ribosome start in the middle.

A plasmid is a small circle of DNA you put into cells so they express a gene. Ours carries the myocardin coding sequence (MYOCD), then an IRES, then GFP.

IRES stands for internal ribosome entry site. Normally a ribosome only starts reading at the front of an mRNA. An IRES is a stretch of RNA that lets a second ribosome land in the middle and start again, so one mRNA makes two separate proteins: myocardin and GFP. Because they come from the same message, a green cell is a cell that is also making myocardin.

The control plasmid, pEGFP, makes GFP only (EGFP is "enhanced" GFP, a brighter version). It shows what transfection plus a foreign fluorescent protein does on its own, with no myocardin.

:::hook
IRES is a second front door. The ribosome walks in the front door to build myocardin, and the IRES lets another ribosome walk in halfway down the hall to build GFP.
:::

### How transfection delivers the plasmid

:::key
- Explain why cells don't take up bare DNA
- List the steps from plasmid to changed gene
*Skim:* Check your manual for the exact reagent names.
:::

The short version: A positively charged lipid reagent ferries the negative plasmid into the cell, which swallows it by endocytosis.

Transfection is a way to put foreign DNA into a cultured cell without a virus. Cells don't take up bare plasmid on their own, because DNA is large and carries many negative charges (from its phosphate backbone), and the cell's outer surface is also negative, so the two repel.

The lab uses a lipid-based reagent to get around that. Positively charged lipids coat the negative plasmid and carry it up to the cell, which takes the package in by endocytosis (the membrane pinches inward to swallow it). Once inside, some plasmid reaches the nucleus, helped along when the nuclear envelope opens during cell division.

Once the plasmid is in the nucleus, this chain lets it change another gene (practice question 2c):

1. The plasmid's promoter is transcribed into MYOCD-IRES-GFP mRNA.
2. The mRNA goes to the cytoplasm.
3. Ribosomes translate it into myocardin and, from the IRES, GFP.
4. Myocardin moves into the nucleus.
5. Myocardin binds SRF sitting on CArG boxes in the cell's own contractile genes.
6. Those genes are transcribed more, making more α-SMA, SM22α, calponin and MHC mRNA.
7. That mRNA is translated into contractile proteins, which reshape the actin.

Step 6 is what ddPCR measures. Step 7 is what phalloidin staining shows.

:::trap
Transfected plasmid usually stays a separate circle in the nucleus and doesn't join the chromosomes. It's slowly lost as cells divide, which is called transient transfection. That's why you read out the experiment a few days later, not weeks later.
:::

### Why GFP is in the experiment

:::key
- State the three jobs GFP does
- Explain the role of the pEGFP control
:::

The short version: GFP is a reporter that glows green with no stain, marking cells that took up the plasmid.

GFP (green fluorescent protein) is a reporter. It glows green on its own, with no stain, so it marks which cells actually took up and expressed the plasmid.

That gives you three things. You can calculate transfection efficiency (green cells ÷ total cells). You can pick out transfected cells when you judge actin shape, so you compare cells that got myocardin against cells that didn't. And the pEGFP control separates myocardin's effect from the effect of just being transfected and making a foreign protein.

:::conn
GFP comes back in Week 6 as the green channel and the numerator of transfection efficiency. pEGFP comes back in Weeks 7 and 8 as one of the three ddPCR conditions (DM, pMyo, pEGFP).
:::

### How to figure it out: plasmid in, contractile genes on {#howto-l3}

Work it in this order:

1. Lipid reagent carries plasmid into the cell
2. Plasmid reaches the nucleus
3. Promoter makes MYOCD-IRES-GFP mRNA
4. Ribosomes build myocardin and GFP
5. Myocardin enters the nucleus
6. Myocardin binds SRF on CArG boxes
7. Contractile genes transcribed more

:::trap
Where it usually breaks: Drawing myocardin on DNA directly (it binds SRF); Forgetting GFP shares the mRNA.
:::

:::hook
SRF is the parked car; myocardin floors it.
:::
