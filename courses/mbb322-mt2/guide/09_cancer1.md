## Lecture 9: cancer I, what a cancer cell is {#l9}

Textbook chapter 20. This lecture treats cancer as a cell biology problem: what's different about cancer cells, and how a tumor builds up over time.

:::story
Chapter 5. Now we break things. This chapter describes what a cancer cell is and how one cell turns into a spreading, deadly mass over years. It sets up the question the next two chapters answer: which genes have to break for this to happen?

What you need from before: growth signals (Lecture 8), proteins tagged with ubiquitin for destruction, the basal lamina and cell adhesion (Midterm 1).
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Your body keeps every tissue the right size by balancing new cells against cells that retire or die on purpose. Cancer is what happens when one cell breaks that balance, and its offspring keep picking up more broken parts over many years, a bit like evolution happening inside one body. The cells stop listening to "stop" signals, eat sugar in a weird way, stop running out of divisions, grow their own blood supply, and finally break out of their home tissue and spread. Most of this lecture is a description of those changes and the experiments that proved them.

:::hook
The life story of a tumor: **"One cell, many hits, blood supply, break out."** One cell starts it (clonal origin), it needs many mutations (multi-hit), it needs blood vessels to get past 2 mm (angiogenesis), and it kills by spreading (metastasis).
:::

### Two defining properties

:::key
- Uncontrolled proliferation + invasion/metastasis
- More division OR less apoptosis can both make a tumor
- Cancer cells escape replicative senescence

*Skim:* The six hallmarks list: know them, but questions usually test the two properties.
:::

The short version: cancer cells divide when they shouldn't, and they invade places they shouldn't.

Cancer cells are defined by two things:

**Property 1: uncontrolled proliferation (division).** Normally a tissue stays the same size because new cells are balanced by cells becoming specialized (differentiation) and cells dying on purpose. That planned, tidy cell suicide is called **apoptosis**. Her figure shows three cases:

| Division | Apoptosis | Result |
|---|---|---|
| normal | normal | balance (**homeostasis**) |
| more | normal | tumor |
| normal | less | tumor |

Either more births or fewer deaths grows a tumor, like a bathtub overflowing because the tap is up or because the drain is clogged. Cancer cells also escape **replicative senescence**, the built-in limit on how many times a cell can divide (explained below).

**Property 2: invasion and colonization of other tissues**, which is **metastasis**.

{{slide:L9-2|Three rows: balance, more division, less apoptosis. The last two both end in a tumor.}}

:::hook
**Bathtub rule:** a tumor fills up when the **tap is too high** (more division) **or the drain is clogged** (less apoptosis).
:::

The "hallmarks" figure on her intro slide lists six abilities a cancer gains:

1. makes its own growth signals (self-sufficiency in growth signals)
2. ignores "stop growing" signals (insensitivity to antigrowth signals)
3. avoids dying on purpose (evasion of apoptosis)
4. never runs out of divisions (limitless replicative potential)
5. grows its own blood vessels (sustained angiogenesis)
6. invades and spreads (tissue invasion and metastasis)

{{slide:L9-1|The six hallmarks around one tumor. Every one of them maps onto a signaling idea in this unit.}}

:::hook
Six hallmarks as one sentence: **"Go, Ignore stop, Never die, Never age, Feed, Spread."**
:::

:::trap
A tumor can grow from either side of the balance. If a question says apoptosis is reduced and division is normal, that's still a tumor.
:::

:::nancy
She writes "Avoid replicative cell senescence."

Plain version: cancer cells never run out of divisions (because they keep telomerase on).
:::

### Oncogenesis

:::key
- Microevolution, years to decades, many accumulated mutations
:::

The short version: cancer forms slowly, by natural selection among your own cells.

**Oncogenesis** (onco = tumor, genesis = making) is the process of forming cancer. Her three points:

- It's a **microevolutionary** process. Cells with mutations that help them out-grow their neighbors take over, the same way natural selection works on animals, but inside one body.
- It takes **years to decades**.
- It generally needs **many mutations piling up** that let cells escape the normal limits on growth.

:::hook
Think of a tumor as a weed that keeps mutating until it beats every other plant in the garden. Slow, many steps, survival of the "fittest" cell.
:::

### Tumors: benign vs malignant

:::key
- Benign stays local above the basal lamina; malignant crosses it
- Invasiveness defines cancer; metastases usually kill
:::

The short version: benign stays home, malignant breaks out. The line it breaks is the basal lamina.

A **tumor** (also called a **neoplasm**, "new growth") is a group of cells all descended from one cell (a **clone**) that keep dividing when they shouldn't.

| | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| Where the cells are | stay together in one local lump in their own tissue | **invade** nearby tissue and spread to other sites (**metastasize**) |
| Basal lamina | stays inside it | breaks through it |
| Why it's dangerous | only if it gets big enough to press on things or makes too much of a hormone or similar substance | invasion is the essential feature of cancer; the spread tumors (metastases), not the first tumor, usually kill |

In her figure: a normal duct is a tidy ring of epithelial cells sitting on a **basal lamina** (also called the basement membrane: a thin sheet of extracellular matrix under every epithelium, like a floor mat). A benign tumor fills up the duct but stays above the mat. A malignant tumor breaks through the mat.

{{slide:L9-3|The line that matters is the yellow basal lamina: benign stays inside it, malignant breaks through.}}

:::hook
**Benign = Behaves** (stays home). **Malignant = Moves** (breaks out through the basal lamina). Size doesn't decide it; the floor mat does.
:::

:::nancy
She writes that benign tumors are only a problem if they "secrete excess amounts of biologically active substances," and defines a tumor as cells that proliferate "in defiance of normal restraints on cell division."

Plain version: benign tumors matter if they get big or make too much of a hormone or similar. A tumor is cells dividing while ignoring the stop signals.
:::

### Naming cancers by tissue

:::key
- Carcinoma = epithelial, ~80%, because epithelia divide a lot
- Sarcoma = connective/muscle; leukemia/lymphoma = blood-forming
- -oma benign vs -carcinoma/-sarcoma malignant
:::

The short version: the name tells you which tissue it came from and whether it's benign.

| Name | Comes from | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Carcinoma** | epithelial cells (cells lining surfaces and organs) | most common, about 80% of human cancers |
| **Sarcoma** | connective tissue or muscle | |
| **Leukemia, lymphoma** | blood-forming (hematopoietic) cells | |

Why are carcinomas so common? Cancer mutations only cause cancer in cells that **divide**, and epithelia divide a lot (gut lining, skin, airways are always being replaced).

Benign and malignant name pairs:

| Tissue | Benign | Malignant |
|---|---|---|
| gland-like epithelium | **adenoma** | **adenocarcinoma** |
| cartilage | **chondroma** | **chondrosarcoma** |

:::easy
Naming trap: adenoma (benign) vs adenocarcinoma (malignant); chondroma (benign) vs chondrosarcoma (malignant). One word part changes the answer, the same way "sialic acid" changed cerebroside to ganglioside on MT1.
:::

:::hook
"**-oma alone = okay-ish (benign).** Add **carcin** (epithelium) or **sarc** (connective tissue) and it's malignant." Carcinoma sounds like "car crash in the lining," sarcoma like "sarcasm hits the bones."
:::

### Normal cells vs cancer cells in a dish

:::key
- Cancer cells: pile into foci, no contact inhibition, divide forever, no growth factor needed

*Skim:* Microscope features list: skim.
:::

The short version: normal cells are polite and stop; cancer cells keep piling up and ignore the rules.

| Normal cells | Malignant cells |
|---|---|
| divide a limited number of times | can divide forever |
| form one flat layer (a **monolayer**) and stop when they touch each other (**contact inhibition**) | pile on top of each other in clumps (**foci**) |
| need growth factors in the liquid | keep growing without growth factors |
| obey "stop growing" signals | ignore them |

Under the microscope, cancer cells look like cells in a rush:

- a big nucleus compared with the rest of the cell (high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio)
- nuclei of uneven size and shape
- obvious **nucleoli** (the spots in the nucleus where ribosomes are made, because they're making lots of protein)
- many cells in the middle of dividing
- few specialized features
- a messy layout with fuzzy borders

{{slide:L9-5|Normal cells stop at one layer; transformed cells pile into foci.}}

:::hook
Normal cells are people on a crowded bus who stop pushing once they touch. Cancer cells keep climbing on each other's shoulders.
:::

### The Warburg effect

:::key
- High glucose uptake, glycolysis for BUILDING BLOCKS, little ox-phos
- Happens even with oxygen
- PET with FDG finds tumors
:::

The short version: cancer cells burn sugar the fast, wasteful way, because what they really want are the leftover building blocks.

Normally a cell breaks glucose in two stages: **glycolysis** (glucose → pyruvate, quick, little energy) and then **oxidative phosphorylation** in the mitochondria (slow, lots of ATP). Cancer cells:

- take up huge amounts of glucose
- run glycolysis very fast
- send very little through oxidative phosphorylation
- turn lots of pyruvate into **lactate**
- use the half-processed pieces of glycolysis as building blocks for new DNA, proteins and lipids

This changed sugar use is the **Warburg effect**.

A **PET scan** uses it: the patient gets radioactive glucose called **fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)**, and tumors light up because they soak it up.

{{slide:L9-7|Compare the thick red arrow into glycolysis on the tumor side with the thin one into oxidative phosphorylation.}}

{{fig:warburg}}

:::hook
Cancer cells "eat fast food": quick, wasteful glycolysis instead of the full mitochondrial meal, because what they really want is the raw material to build more of themselves. Think of a builder who strips the wood off a house for lumber instead of living in it.
:::

:::trap
Warburg cells aren't short on oxygen when they do this. They choose glycolysis even when oxygen is there. The point is building blocks for growth, not ATP.
:::

### New metabolites: 2-hydroxyglutarate

:::key
- Mutant IDH makes 2-HG, which blocks α-ketoglutarate enzymes that control genes

*Skim:* 70% / 25% figures: recognize.
:::

The short version: one broken metabolism enzyme makes a new chemical that jams the enzymes controlling genes.

About **70% of certain brain cancers (gliomas)** and about **25% of acute myeloid leukemias (AML)** have a mutant **isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)**, an enzyme in the citric acid cycle (the mitochondria's main fuel-burning loop). The mutant enzyme makes a new chemical, **2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG)**, which builds up to high levels.

2-HG looks a lot like a normal molecule called α-ketoglutarate, so it jams many enzymes that need α-ketoglutarate to work. Many of those enzymes control which genes are on (for example, enzymes that remove methyl tags from histones and DNA, called demethylases). So a metabolism mutation ends up changing gene expression.

:::hook
"**IDH makes a bad copy of a key.**" 2-HG looks like α-ketoglutarate, slides into the lock and jams it. "**I**DH: **I**n gliomas (70%) and AML (25%)."
:::

:::nancy
She writes "70% of brain cancers... have a mutation in isocitrate dehydrogenase."

Plain version: the real figure applies to certain brain cancers (lower-grade gliomas), not all of them. On her exam, though, "70%" with "brain cancers" is what she expects.
:::

### Telomeres and replicative senescence

:::key
- Telomeres (TTAGGG) shorten each division; telomerase adds them back
- Somatic cells: little telomerase → senescence (25-50 doublings)
- Cancer cells keep telomerase on
:::

The short version: chromosome tips shorten each division until the cell retires. Cancer cells keep a repair enzyme switched on so they never retire.

**Telomeres** are repeats of a short DNA sequence (**TTAGGG** in humans) at the ends of our chromosomes. The normal copying machinery can't copy the very tip of a linear chromosome, so every division the telomere gets a little shorter. The enzyme **telomerase** adds TTAGGG repeats back on.

| Cell type | Telomerase |
|---|---|
| embryonic cells, germ cells (precursors of eggs and sperm), stem cells | made |
| most dividing body (somatic) cells | very little |
| cancer cells | kept active |

So in most body cells, telomeres shrink each cell cycle, and the cell stops dividing after a set number of divisions. This built-in counter is **replicative senescence**. Human fibroblasts stop after **25-50 doublings**. Cancer cells escape this by keeping telomerase on.

{{fig:telomere}}

:::hook
Telomeres are the plastic tips (aglets) on a shoelace. Each division snips a bit off; when the tip is gone, the lace frays and the cell retires. Telomerase is the aglet-repair kit that cancer cells refuse to give up. "**TTAGGG**" is "**T**wo **T**imes **A** **G**ood **G**ame **G**one," a little shorter each round.
:::

### Clonal origin

:::key
- A tumor comes from ONE cell
- Proof: same 9;22 breakpoint (Philadelphia) in every CML cell
- One mutation is still not enough
:::

The short version: every cell in a tumor comes from one original cell, and the proof is that they all share the same chromosome break.

Even after a cancer spreads, it can usually be traced back to a single first (**primary**) tumor in one organ, and that tumor started from **one** cell with a change it passed to its offspring. This is **clonal origin**.

The evidence: in **chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML)**, two chromosomes (9 and 22) swap pieces. This swap is a **reciprocal translocation**. It leaves a short chromosome 22 called the **Philadelphia chromosome**. Every leukemia cell in a patient has the **exact same** break point, which means they all came from one ancestor cell. (If the breaks happened separately in many cells, they'd be at slightly different spots.)

But one mutation isn't enough to make a normal cell cancerous (next section).

:::hook
Same break in every cell = same family. It's like every cousin having the exact same chipped tooth: they inherited it from one grandparent.
:::

:::conn
The 9;22 translocation makes the Bcr-Abl fusion gene, which shows up in Lecture 10 as the target of the drug Gleevec. Same patient, same chromosome, now seen as a signaling problem.
:::

### The multi-hit model

:::key
- Several mutations needed over years
- Evidence: age curve, myc + rasD mice, colon steps
- Colon order: APC → K-ras → Smad4 (chr 18) → p53 (p53 is LATE)
:::

The short version: you need several mutations stacked up in one cell's line before you get cancer, and that takes years.

One mutation is generally not enough. After the first one, more changes pile up in some of that cell's offspring. Each change helps them out-grow, out-divide and outlive their neighbors, until eventually they can spread. By the time a tumor is found, it's usually been growing for many years and has a billion or more cells. Cancer usually starts in cells that divide.

{{slide:L9-11|One cell, then 2, then 3 mutations, each step giving a bigger, more aggressive clone.}}

{{fig:multihit}}

:::hook
Cancer is like a car crash that needs several failures at once: a stuck gas pedal (oncogene), broken brakes (tumor suppressor), and a mechanic who stopped fixing things (DNA repair). One failure alone rarely causes a crash.
:::

Her three pieces of evidence:

1. Cancer is mostly a disease of old age. On a log-log plot, cancer rates rise in a steep straight line, about the 5th to 6th power of age. That's what you'd expect if you need several separate hits, each of which takes time.
2. In mice, mutations team up. Mice engineered to carry only *myc* or only *rasD* get tumors slowly. Mice with both get tumors much faster, and almost all of them do.
3. Colon cancer goes in steps you can trace:

| Step | What breaks | Chromosome | What you see |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | lose **APC** (tumor suppressor) | 5 | normal lining → small polyp |
| 2 | turn on **K-ras** (oncogene) | 12 | benign adenoma grows |
| 3 | lose a tumor suppressor near **DCC** (Smad4) | 18 | larger adenoma |
| 4 | lose **p53** (tumor suppressor) | 17 | malignant carcinoma |
| 5 | other changes | | invasion and metastasis |

{{slide:L9-13|Read the blue boxes top to bottom for the mutation order: APC, K-ras, chr18, p53.}}

{{slide:L9-12|Bottom graph: myc + rasD mice lose tumor-free status far faster than either single transgenic.}}

:::trap
The colon order is APC first, then K-ras, then chr 18 (DCC/Smad4), then p53. Students often put p53 first because it's famous. It comes late, at the step to malignancy. Mnemonic: "**A Kid Should Panic**" (APC, K-ras, Smad4 on chr 18, p53).
:::

:::hook
Chromosome numbers in order: **5, 12, 18, 17.** "A Kid Should Panic at **5**, **12**, **18**, then **17** feels too late." Only the last one goes backwards.
:::

:::conn
What's on chromosome 18? Lecture 11 answers it: Smad4 and Smad2, the messengers of the TGFβ "stop dividing" signal. Her updated figure in Lecture 11 renames this step "loss of Smad4 and other tumor suppressors."
:::

:::nancy
Her Lecture 9 colon figure labels the chromosome 18 step "loss of tumor suppressor in DCC region." Her Lecture 11 figure renames the same step "loss of Smad4 and other tumor suppressors."

Plain version: same step, two names. If you see "DCC region," think Smad4 on chromosome 18.
:::

### Genetic instability

:::key
- Cancer cells mutate faster, often from broken DNA-maintenance genes
- Leads to chaotic karyotypes and heterogeneity
:::

The short version: cancer cells have broken spell-checkers, so they collect mutations faster, which speeds everything up.

Human cancer cells are **genetically unstable**: they pick up genetic changes faster than normal cells, often because they can't fix DNA damage or copying mistakes. Usually that's because the genes that look after DNA are themselves mutated. More mutations means more chances to hit the next gene the tumor needs, so instability speeds up the whole process.

A cancer **karyotype** (a picture of all a cell's chromosomes) looks chaotic:

- point mutations (single-letter changes)
- amplifications (extra copies of a region)
- deletions (missing pieces)
- the wrong number of chromosomes, whole or partial

Because the genome keeps changing, different cells in the same tumor end up with different mutations. That's called **heterogeneity**.

:::hook
Genetic instability is a broken spell-checker. Typos pile up faster, and every so often a typo happens to help the tumor.
:::

### Metastasis

:::key
- Normal displaced cells die (lose anchorage signals); cancer cells extrude basally and survive
- Steps: lose adhesion, cross basal lamina, enter blood, exit, colonize
- Under 1/1000 survive the blood; metastasis = ~90% of deaths
:::

The short version: let go, break through the floor, ride the blood, get off somewhere new, and grow. Almost every cell dies trying, but the few that make it cause most cancer deaths.

**How cancer cells survive leaving home.** Normally an epithelial cell that gets knocked out of place is pushed into the hollow middle of the tube (the lumen). This is **apical extrusion** ("pushed out the top"). It dies there, because it loses **anchorage-dependent survival signals** (the "you're attached, stay alive" signals cells get from touching the matrix) and goes into apoptosis. A cancer cell can instead push out the bottom (**basal extrusion**) into the tissue, and it survives because it turns up its own survival signals and dodges apoptosis.

{{slide:L9-16|Normal cells extrude into the lumen and die; the tumor cell extrudes basally and survives.}}

:::hook
Normal cell falls **up** into the tube and dies. Cancer cell falls **down** into the tissue and lives.
:::

**The steps of metastasis:**

1. Let go of neighbors (lose cell-cell adhesion).
2. Break out through the basal lamina.
3. Hop on: enter a blood vessel (**intravasation**, "into the vessel").
4. Ride through the bloodstream. Fewer than 1 in 1000 cells survive this.
5. Hop off: stick to a vessel wall in a distant organ (like the liver) and squeeze out.
6. Settle in: grow into a tiny tumor (micrometastasis), then a full metastasis.

Very few cells succeed at starting a new tumor, but metastasis causes about **90% of cancer deaths**.

{{slide:L9-17|The full journey, including the "fewer than 1 in 1000 cells will survive" note in the bloodstream.}}

{{fig:metastasis}}

{{3d:met|Benign cells pile up above the basal lamina; the malignant one breaks through, rides the blood and starts a new tumor.}}

:::hook
"**Let go, Break out, Hop on, Ride, Hop off, Settle in.**" Six steps. And two numbers: **under 1 in 1000 survive the ride**, but metastasis causes **90% of deaths**.
:::

:::nancy
She uses "basement membrane" on the metastasis slides and "basal lamina" elsewhere.

Plain version: they're the same thin floor mat under the epithelium.
:::

### The tumor microenvironment

:::key
- Tumors recruit normal support cells; the support tissue is the stroma
:::

The short version: a tumor isn't only cancer cells; it recruits normal neighbors that help it.

Tumors also contain normal support cells:

- fibroblasts (connective tissue cells)
- endothelial cells and pericytes (which build blood vessels)
- lymphatic cells
- white blood cells
- extracellular matrix

The supporting connective tissue around a tumor is called the **stroma**. These neighbors affect how the cancer develops.

### Angiogenesis

:::key
- No vessels = tumor stuck at ~2 mm
- Tumors secrete bFGF, TGFα, VEGF
- Thyroid and eye experiments: blood supply alone decides growth
:::

The short version: without its own blood supply, a tumor stalls at about 2 mm. Tumors send out signals that make blood vessels grow toward them.

**Angiogenesis** means growing new blood vessels (angio = vessel, genesis = making). Without vessels bringing food and oxygen, a tumor can only reach about **2 mm** across. The cells in the middle die (that's **necrosis**, messy unplanned death).

Many tumors release growth factors that trigger angiogenesis:

- **bFGF** (basic fibroblast growth factor)
- **TGFα** (transforming growth factor alpha)
- **VEGF** (vascular endothelial growth factor)

The lining cells of a nearby tiny blood vessel (capillary) send out a sprout. The sprout hollows into a new tube and grows into the tumor. More blood means more growth, which means more divisions and more chances for harmful mutations.

:::hook
A tumor is a city that needs roads. At 2 mm it runs out of delivery trucks, so it builds highways (vessels) by shouting VEGF. "**2 mm without a highway.**"
:::

:::she
"Two experiments illustrating the requirement for angiogenesis."
:::

1. Thyroid experiment. Cancer cells were injected into a thyroid gland that had been removed and kept alive by pumping in nutrient liquid (so it had no working blood vessels). The tumors stopped growing at 1-2 mm.
2. Eye experiment. Tumor cells floating in the fluid of the front chamber of the eye (no blood vessels) stayed tiny. Tumor cells placed on the iris (which has blood vessels) grew fast, with volume shooting up within days.

Both say the same thing: same tumor cells, the only difference is the blood supply, and that decides growth.

{{slide:L9-21|Left: the perfused thyroid caps tumor size at 1-2 mm. Right: iris-grown tumor (red line) shoots up, anterior chamber tumor stays flat.}}

:::hook
"**Thyroid in a jar, eye with and without a road.**" Two setups, same answer: no blood, no growth.
:::

### Hypoxia, HIF and VEGF

:::key
- Low O2: HIF active → VEGF
- High O2: HIF hydroxylated → E3 ligase (VHL) → proteasome
- VHL syndrome: HIF not destroyed → hemangioblastomas
:::

The short version: HIF is the "we need oxygen" alarm. Oxygen normally tags HIF for the trash. When oxygen is low, HIF survives and calls for blood vessels.

When oxygen is low (**hypoxia**), a transcription factor called **HIF** (hypoxia-inducible factor) becomes active and switches on **VEGF** and other genes needed when oxygen is scarce. VEGF draws in new blood vessels.

When oxygen is plentiful:

1. HIF gets **hydroxylated** (an -OH group added) on certain prolines (an amino acid).
2. The hydroxylated HIF is recognized by an **E3 ubiquitin ligase**, the enzyme that sticks chains of ubiquitin on proteins to mark them for destruction.
3. The marked HIF is shredded by the **proteasome** (the cell's protein shredder). This route is the **ubiquitin-proteasome pathway**.

So oxygen keeps HIF levels low.

| | Normal oxygen | Low oxygen (hypoxia) |
|---|---|---|
| HIF prolines | hydroxylated | not hydroxylated |
| E3 ligase (with VHL) | grabs HIF | can't grab it |
| HIF | destroyed | builds up, active |
| VEGF | off | on, new vessels grow |

{{fig:hif}}

{{lab:hif|HIF / VHL / VEGF (hypoxia, VHL syndrome)}}

:::she
Human Perspective: von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome. The VHL protein is a subunit of the E3 ubiquitin ligase that destroys HIF. When it's defective, cells make large amounts of HIF regardless of oxygen. The result is **hemangioblastomas**, tumors packed with dense masses of blood vessels.
:::

:::trap
In VHL, HIF isn't overproduced by more transcription; it just isn't destroyed. Broken OFF switch again, so the cell "thinks" it's always low on oxygen and keeps making VEGF.
:::

:::hook
**HIF = "Help, I'm Fainting!"** It only shouts for blood vessels when oxygen is low. Oxygen puts the -OH "trash tag" on it; **VHL is the garbage collector**. Lose the garbage collector and the alarm never stops, so you get tumors full of blood vessels.
:::

:::nancy
For VHL syndrome she writes that cells "produce large quantities of HIF regardless of O2 levels" (and spells it "Hippel-Landau").

Plain version: HIF isn't being made faster. It just isn't being destroyed, because VHL (the garbage collector) is broken. The name is von Hippel-Lindau.
:::
