## Lecture 9: cancer I, what a cancer cell is {#l9}

Textbook chapter 20. This lecture treats cancer as a cell biology problem: what's different about cancer cells, and how a tumor builds up over time.

:::story
Chapter 5. Now we break things. This chapter describes what a cancer cell is and how one cell turns into a spreading, deadly mass over years. It sets up the question the next two chapters answer: which genes have to break for this to happen?

What you need from before: growth signals (Lecture 8), proteins tagged with ubiquitin for destruction, the basal lamina and cell adhesion (Midterm 1).
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

Your body keeps every tissue the right size. New cells are made, and old cells retire or die on purpose, and the two stay balanced. Cancer starts when one cell breaks that balance. Its offspring then keep picking up more broken parts over many years, a bit like evolution happening inside one body. Along the way the cells stop listening to "stop" signals, burn sugar in an odd way, never run out of divisions, grow their own blood supply, and finally break out of their home tissue and spread. This lecture describes each of those changes and the experiments that proved them.

:::hook
The life story of a tumor: **"One cell, many hits, blood supply, break out."** It starts from one cell (clonal origin). It needs many mutations (multi-hit). It needs blood vessels to grow past 2 mm (angiogenesis). And it kills by spreading (metastasis).
:::

### Two defining properties

:::key
- Cancer cells do two things normal cells don't: divide out of control, and invade other tissues
- A tumor grows if cells divide too much OR die too little. Either one is enough
- Cancer cells never hit the normal limit on how many times a cell can divide

*Skim:* The six hallmarks list: know them, but questions usually test the two properties.
:::

The short version: cancer cells divide when they shouldn't, and they invade places they shouldn't.

Every cancer cell has two defining properties.

**Property 1: they divide out of control.** The word for cell division is **proliferation**.

To see why that matters, picture how a healthy tissue stays the same size. Three things are always happening:

- New cells are being made by division.
- Some cells mature into their final job and stop dividing (**differentiation**).
- Some cells kill themselves on purpose in a clean, planned way. That planned cell suicide is called **apoptosis**.

When births and deaths match, the tissue stays the same size. That steady balance is **homeostasis**. Her figure shows what happens when the balance tips:

| Cell division | Cell death (apoptosis) | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| normal | normal | the tissue stays the same size (homeostasis) |
| too much | normal | a tumor, because too many new cells are made |
| normal | too little | a tumor, because old cells don't die when they should |

Cancer cells also escape **replicative senescence**. That's the normal limit on how many times a cell can divide before it retires. (Details in the telomere lesson.)

**Property 2: they invade other tissues and set up new colonies there.** That spread is called **metastasis**.

{{slide:L9-2|Three rows: balance, more division, less apoptosis. The last two both end in a tumor.}}

:::hook
**Bathtub rule:** a bathtub overflows if the **tap is turned up** (more division) **or the drain is clogged** (less apoptosis). A tumor is an overflowing tub.
:::

Her intro slide also shows the six "hallmarks" of cancer, six abilities a cancer cell gains along the way:

1. It makes its own "grow" signals, so it doesn't need anyone else's.
2. It ignores "stop growing" signals.
3. It avoids apoptosis (it won't kill itself when it should).
4. It never runs out of divisions.
5. It gets new blood vessels to grow toward it (angiogenesis).
6. It invades nearby tissue and spreads (metastasis).

{{slide:L9-1|The six hallmarks around one tumor. Every one of them maps onto a signaling idea in this unit.}}

:::hook
Six hallmarks as one sentence: **"Go, Ignore stop, Never die, Never age, Feed, Spread."**
:::

:::trap
A tumor can grow from either side of the balance. If a question says apoptosis is reduced and division is normal, that's still a tumor.
:::

:::nancy
She writes "Avoid replicative cell senescence."

Plain version: cancer cells never run out of divisions, because they keep the enzyme telomerase switched on.
:::

### Oncogenesis

:::key
- Cancer forms by natural selection among your own cells
- It takes years to decades
- It needs many mutations to pile up in one line of cells
:::

The short version: cancer forms slowly, by natural selection among your own cells.

**Oncogenesis** means the making of a cancer ("onco" = tumor, "genesis" = making). She makes three points about it.

1. It's **microevolution**. Inside your body, a cell gets a mutation that helps it divide a little faster than its neighbors. Its offspring take over more space. One of them gets another helpful mutation and takes over again. That's natural selection, except the competitors are your own cells.
2. It's slow. It takes **years to decades**.
3. It usually needs **many mutations**, each one removing another normal limit on growth.

:::hook
Think of a tumor as a weed that keeps mutating until it beats every other plant in the garden. Slow, many steps, survival of the "fittest" cell.
:::

### Tumors: benign vs malignant

:::key
- A tumor is a lump of cells that all came from one cell and keep dividing when they shouldn't
- Benign tumors stay in place, on their own side of the basal lamina
- Malignant tumors break through the basal lamina and spread. That's what makes something "cancer"
- People rarely die from the first tumor. They die from the new tumors it seeds elsewhere
:::

The short version: benign stays home, malignant breaks out. The line it breaks through is the basal lamina.

The words you need:

- A **tumor** is a lump of cells that keep dividing when they shouldn't. All its cells come from one starting cell. Another name for a tumor is a **neoplasm** ("new growth").
- The **basal lamina** is a thin sheet of extracellular matrix that every epithelium (lining tissue) sits on, like a floor mat under the cells. It's also called the **basement membrane**.
- **Invade** means push into the surrounding tissue.
- **Metastasize** means travel to another part of the body and start a new tumor there. The new tumor is called a **metastasis** (plural **metastases**).

There are two kinds of tumor:

| | Benign tumor | Malignant tumor (cancer) |
|---|---|---|
| Does it stay in one place? | Yes. Its cells stay together in one lump, in the tissue where it started. | No. Its cells push into nearby tissue and can travel to other organs. |
| What happens at the basal lamina? | The cells stay on their own side of it. | The cells break through it. |
| When is it dangerous? | Only if it grows big enough to press on something important, or if it makes too much of a hormone or another active chemical. | Always. The first tumor is rarely what kills a patient. The new tumors it seeds around the body (metastases) are what usually kill. |

So invasion is the feature that makes a tumor a cancer. Size isn't.

In her figure, a normal duct (tube) is a neat ring of epithelial cells sitting on the basal lamina. A benign tumor fills up the inside of the duct but stays above the mat. A malignant tumor breaks through the mat into the tissue underneath.

{{slide:L9-3|The line that matters is the yellow basal lamina: benign stays inside it, malignant breaks through.}}

:::hook
**Benign = Behaves** (stays home). **Malignant = Moves** (breaks out through the basal lamina). Size doesn't decide it; the floor mat does.
:::

:::nancy
She writes that benign tumors are only a problem if they "secrete excess amounts of biologically active substances," and defines a tumor as cells that proliferate "in defiance of normal restraints on cell division."

Plain version: a benign tumor matters if it gets big or makes too much of a hormone or similar chemical. A tumor is cells that keep dividing while ignoring the stop signals.
:::

### Naming cancers by tissue

:::key
- Carcinoma: from epithelium (lining cells). About 80% of cancers, because lining cells divide a lot
- Sarcoma: from connective tissue or muscle. Leukemia and lymphoma: from blood-forming cells
- A name ending in plain "-oma" is usually benign; "-carcinoma" or "-sarcoma" means malignant
:::

The short version: a cancer's name tells you which tissue it came from and whether it's benign.

| Name | Where it starts | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Carcinoma** | epithelial cells, the cells that line surfaces and organs (skin, gut, airways) | the most common kind, about 80% of human cancers |
| **Sarcoma** | connective tissue (like bone and cartilage) or muscle | much less common than carcinomas |
| **Leukemia** and **lymphoma** | blood-forming cells (the word for these is **hematopoietic**) | leukemia shows up in the blood, lymphoma in the lymph nodes |

Why are carcinomas so common? A mutation can only turn into cancer in a cell that divides. Lining cells divide constantly, because your gut lining, skin and airways are always being replaced. More divisions mean more chances for a cancer-causing mistake.

Benign and malignant tumors from the same tissue get matching names:

| Tissue | Benign name | Malignant name |
|---|---|---|
| gland-like epithelium | **adenoma** | **adenocarcinoma** |
| cartilage | **chondroma** | **chondrosarcoma** |

:::easy
Naming trap: adenoma is benign but adenocarcinoma is malignant; chondroma is benign but chondrosarcoma is malignant. One extra word part changes the answer, the same way "sialic acid" changed cerebroside into ganglioside on MT1.
:::

:::hook
"**-oma alone = okay-ish (benign).** Add **carcin** (epithelium) or **sarc** (connective tissue) and it's malignant." Carcinoma sounds like "car crash in the lining," sarcoma like "sarcasm hits the bones."
:::

### Normal cells vs cancer cells in a dish

:::key
- Normal cells grow into one flat layer and stop when they touch (contact inhibition)
- Cancer cells keep dividing, pile on top of each other into clumps (foci), divide forever, and don't need growth factors

*Skim:* Microscope features list: skim.
:::

The short version: in a dish, normal cells are polite and stop when the space is full. Cancer cells keep piling up and ignore the rules.

The words you need:

- A **monolayer** is a single flat layer of cells, one cell thick.
- **Contact inhibition** is the rule that normal cells stop dividing once they touch their neighbors on all sides.
- **Foci** (singular **focus**) are clumps where cells have piled on top of each other.
- A **transformed** cell is a cell grown in the lab that has picked up cancer-like behavior.

| Normal cells in a dish | Cancer (transformed) cells in a dish |
|---|---|
| divide a limited number of times, then stop for good | can keep dividing forever |
| form a single flat layer and stop dividing once they touch each other | keep dividing after they touch and pile up into clumps (foci) |
| only divide when growth factors are added to the liquid | keep dividing with little or no growth factor |
| obey "stop growing" signals | ignore "stop growing" signals |

Under the microscope, cancer cells look like cells in a rush:

- The nucleus is big compared with the rest of the cell.
- The nuclei come in uneven sizes and shapes.
- The **nucleoli** stand out. These are the spots in the nucleus that build ribosomes, and they're busy because the cell is making lots of protein.
- Many cells are caught in the middle of dividing.
- The cells have lost most of their specialized features.
- The tissue looks messy, with fuzzy borders.

{{slide:L9-5|Normal cells stop at one layer; transformed cells pile into foci.}}

:::exam
Her Q5 describes a "wound" test: scrape a circle of cells out of a full monolayer. Normal cells at the edge divide to fill the gap and then stop when they touch again, because of contact inhibition.

Model answer for transformed cells: they fill the gap too, but they don't stop when they meet. They've lost contact inhibition, so they keep dividing, pile on top of each other into a multilayer and form clumps (foci). They also need much less growth factor to keep going.
:::

:::hook
Normal cells are people on a crowded bus who stop pushing once they touch. Cancer cells keep climbing on each other's shoulders.
:::

### The Warburg effect

:::key
- Cancer cells take up huge amounts of sugar and break it down the fast, low-energy way (glycolysis)
- They do this even when oxygen is available. They want the half-broken sugar pieces as building materials
- A PET scan with radioactive sugar (FDG) lights up tumors because of this
:::

The short version: cancer cells burn sugar the fast, wasteful way, because what they really want are the leftover pieces to build new cells.

First, how a normal cell gets energy from glucose (sugar). It works in two stages:

1. **Glycolysis** happens in the cytosol. It splits glucose into a smaller molecule called pyruvate. It's fast but makes only a little ATP (energy).
2. **Oxidative phosphorylation** happens in the mitochondria and needs oxygen. It burns pyruvate all the way down and makes lots of ATP. It's slower.

A normal cell with oxygen sends most of its pyruvate on to stage 2. A cancer cell doesn't. Instead it:

- takes up huge amounts of glucose
- runs glycolysis very fast
- sends very little on to the mitochondria
- turns a lot of the pyruvate into **lactate** and gets rid of it
- pulls out the half-broken pieces of sugar along the way and uses them to build new DNA, proteins and fats

That switch is called the **Warburg effect**. It makes sense for a cell whose goal is to divide: making a new cell takes building materials, not only energy.

Doctors use this to find tumors. In a **PET scan**, the patient is given radioactive glucose called **fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)**. Tumors soak up far more glucose than normal tissue, so they light up on the scan.

{{slide:L9-7|Compare the thick red arrow into glycolysis on the tumor side with the thin one into oxidative phosphorylation.}}

{{fig:warburg}}

:::hook
Cancer cells "eat fast food": quick, wasteful glycolysis instead of the full mitochondrial meal, because what they really want is raw material to build more of themselves. Think of a builder who tears the wood off a house to use as lumber instead of living in it.

:::trap
Warburg cells aren't short of oxygen. They choose glycolysis even when oxygen is there. The point is building blocks for growth, not ATP.

### New metabolites: 2-hydroxyglutarate

:::key
- Some brain cancers and leukemias have a broken enzyme called IDH
- Broken IDH makes a new chemical, 2-HG, that looks like α-ketoglutarate
- 2-HG jams the enzymes that control which genes are on

*Skim:* 70% / 25% figures: recognize.
:::

The short version: one broken metabolism enzyme makes a fake chemical that jams the enzymes controlling genes.

The words you need:

- **Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)** is an enzyme in the citric acid cycle, the main fuel-burning loop inside mitochondria.
- **α-ketoglutarate** is a normal molecule from that cycle. Many enzymes need it as a helper to work.
- **Gliomas** are a type of brain cancer. **Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)** is a type of blood cancer.

About **70% of certain brain cancers (gliomas)** and about **25% of AMLs** carry a mutant IDH. The mutant enzyme makes something new: **2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG)**, which builds up to high levels in the cell.

2-HG is shaped almost like α-ketoglutarate. So it slides into enzymes that need α-ketoglutarate and jams them. Many of those enzymes control which genes are switched on, for example the enzymes that strip methyl tags off DNA and histones (**demethylases**). The result: a mutation in a metabolism enzyme ends up changing which genes the cell uses.

:::hook
"**IDH makes a bad copy of a key.**" 2-HG looks like α-ketoglutarate, slides into the lock and jams it. "**I**DH: **I**n gliomas (70%) and AML (25%)."
:::

:::nancy
She writes "70% of brain cancers... have a mutation in isocitrate dehydrogenase."

Plain version: the real figure applies to certain brain cancers (lower-grade gliomas), not all of them. On her exam, though, "70%" with "brain cancers" is what she expects.
:::

### Telomeres and replicative senescence

:::key
- Telomeres are repeated TTAGGG caps on chromosome ends. They get shorter every division
- Telomerase is the enzyme that adds them back. Most body cells make very little
- When the caps run out, the cell stops dividing for good (senescence, after 25-50 doublings). Cancer cells keep telomerase on so they never stop
:::

The short version: chromosome tips get shorter each division until the cell retires. Cancer cells keep a repair enzyme switched on so they never retire.

The words you need:

- **Telomeres** are the caps on the ends of every chromosome. They're made of a short DNA sequence repeated over and over: **TTAGGG** in humans.
- **Telomerase** is the enzyme that adds TTAGGG repeats back onto the ends.
- **Replicative senescence** means a cell has permanently stopped dividing because it has used up its divisions.

Why do telomeres shrink? The normal DNA-copying machinery can't copy the very tip of a chromosome. So every time a cell divides, its telomeres get a little shorter. Only telomerase can add the lost pieces back.

Which cells make telomerase?

| Cell type | How much telomerase |
|---|---|
| embryo cells, germ cells (the cells that become eggs and sperm), and stem cells | they make it, so their telomeres stay long |
| most ordinary body cells (**somatic** cells) | very little, so their telomeres shrink with each division |
| cancer cells | they keep it switched on, so their telomeres never run out |

In most body cells, the telomeres shrink a bit each division, and after a set number of divisions the cell stops for good. That's replicative senescence, a built-in division counter. Human fibroblasts (connective tissue cells) stop after **25-50 doublings**. Cancer cells escape the counter by keeping telomerase on.

{{fig:telomere}}

:::hook
Telomeres are the plastic tips on a shoelace. Each division snips a bit off. When the tip is gone, the lace frays and the cell retires. Telomerase is the tip-repair kit that cancer cells refuse to give up. "**TTAGGG**" is "**T**wo **T**imes **A** **G**ood **G**ame **G**one," a little shorter each round.
:::

### Clonal origin

:::key
- Every cell in a tumor comes from ONE original cell
- Proof: in CML leukemia, every cancer cell has the exact same chromosome 9-22 break (the Philadelphia chromosome)
- But one mutation alone isn't enough to make that cell cancerous
:::

The short version: every cell in a tumor descends from one original cell, and the proof is that they all share the exact same chromosome break.

Even after a cancer has spread, you can usually trace it back to one first tumor in one organ. That first tumor is the **primary tumor**. And that primary tumor started from **one** cell, which passed its change on to all its offspring. Starting from one cell is called **clonal origin** (a **clone** is a group of cells that all came from one cell).

The proof comes from a blood cancer called **chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML)**:

1. In CML, chromosome 9 and chromosome 22 break, and they swap the broken-off pieces. A two-way swap like this is a **reciprocal translocation**.
2. The swap leaves behind a shortened chromosome 22, called the **Philadelphia chromosome**.
3. In each patient, every leukemia cell has its break at the exact same spot.

Why does that prove one starting cell? If the break had happened separately in lots of cells, each break would land in a slightly different place. The same break everywhere means one cell broke once, and all the others inherited it.

One mutation still isn't enough to make a normal cell cancerous, though. That's the next lesson.

:::hook
Same break in every cell = same family. It's like every cousin having the exact same chipped tooth: they inherited it from one grandparent.
:::

:::conn
The 9-22 swap creates a fused gene called Bcr-Abl, which comes back in Lecture 10 as the target of the drug Gleevec. Same patient, same chromosome, now seen as a signaling problem.
:::

### The multi-hit model

:::key
- Cancer needs several mutations to stack up in one line of cells, which takes years
- Three pieces of evidence: cancer rises steeply with age, mice with two oncogenes get tumors much faster, and colon cancer goes through visible steps
- Colon order: APC, then K-ras, then Smad4 (chromosome 18), then p53. p53 comes LATE
:::

The short version: one mutation isn't enough. Several have to stack up in the same line of cells, and that takes years.

Here's how it builds:

1. One cell gets a first mutation that lets it divide a bit more.
2. Its offspring make a small group. One of them gets a second mutation and does even better.
3. That cell's offspring take over. Another mutation, another takeover.
4. Each step lets the cells out-grow, out-divide and outlive their neighbors, until finally they can invade and spread.

By the time a tumor is found, it has usually been growing for many years and holds a billion or more cells. Cancer usually starts in cells that divide, because dividing is when copying mistakes happen.

{{slide:L9-11|One cell, then 2, then 3 mutations, each step giving a bigger, more aggressive clone.}}

{{fig:multihit}}

:::hook
Cancer is like a car crash that needs several failures at once: a stuck gas pedal (oncogene), broken brakes (tumor suppressor), and a mechanic who stopped fixing things (DNA repair). One failure alone rarely causes a crash.
:::

She gives three pieces of evidence:

1. **Cancer is mostly a disease of old age.** Plotted on a log-log graph, cancer rates climb in a steep straight line, roughly with the 5th to 6th power of age. That's the pattern you'd expect if a cell needs several separate hits and each hit takes time to happen.
2. **Mutations team up in mice.** Mice engineered to carry only the cancer gene *myc*, or only the cancer gene *rasD*, get tumors slowly. Mice carrying both get tumors much faster, and almost all of them get one.
3. **Colon cancer goes through steps you can see.** Each step adds one mutation and one visible change:

| Step | What goes wrong | On chromosome | What the tissue looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | the tumor suppressor **APC** is lost | 5 | the normal lining forms a small bump (a **polyp**) |
| 2 | the oncogene **K-ras** is switched on | 12 | the polyp grows into a benign tumor (an **adenoma**) |
| 3 | a tumor suppressor near a spot called **DCC** is lost (this is **Smad4**) | 18 | the adenoma gets bigger |
| 4 | the tumor suppressor **p53** is lost | 17 | it becomes malignant (a **carcinoma**) |
| 5 | more changes (her figure doesn't name the genes) | not named | it invades and spreads |

{{slide:L9-12|Bottom graph: myc + rasD mice lose tumor-free status far faster than either single transgenic.}}

### Genetic instability

:::key
- Cancer cells collect mutations faster than normal cells
- Usually because the genes that check and repair DNA are themselves broken
- Result: messy chromosomes, and different cells in one tumor carry different mutations (heterogeneity)
:::

The short version: cancer cells have a broken spell-checker, so they collect mistakes faster, which speeds everything up.

Human cancer cells are **genetically unstable**. That means they pick up DNA changes much faster than normal cells. Usually the reason is that the genes whose job is to look after DNA (checking it, repairing damage, fixing copying mistakes) have themselves been mutated. More mistakes mean more chances to hit the next gene the tumor needs, so instability speeds up the whole multi-hit process.

A **karyotype** is a picture of all of a cell's chromosomes laid out in pairs. A cancer cell's karyotype looks chaotic. It can have:

- **point mutations**: single DNA letters changed
- **amplifications**: extra copies of one region
- **deletions**: missing pieces
- the wrong number of chromosomes, or extra or missing chunks of them

Because the DNA keeps changing, different cells inside the same tumor end up with different mutations. That variety is called **heterogeneity**.

:::hook
Genetic instability is a broken spell-checker. Typos pile up faster, and every so often a typo happens to help the tumor.
:::

### Metastasis

:::key
- A normal cell knocked out of place dies. A cancer cell slips out the bottom of the tissue and survives
- Six steps: let go of neighbors, break through the basal lamina, get into a blood vessel, ride the blood, get out in a new organ, grow there
- Fewer than 1 in 1000 cells survive the trip, yet metastasis causes about 90% of cancer deaths
:::

The short version: let go, break through the floor, ride the blood, get off somewhere new, and grow. Almost every cell dies trying, but the few that make it cause most cancer deaths.

**First, why a normal cell can't do this.** Epithelial cells form tubes, like the ducts in her figures. The hollow inside of a tube is the **lumen**.

- When a normal cell gets knocked out of place, it's pushed into the lumen. This is **apical extrusion** ("pushed out the top"). Once it loses contact with the basal lamina, it loses the "you're attached, stay alive" signals it was getting. Those are called **anchorage-dependent survival signals**. Without them, the cell goes into apoptosis and dies.
- A cancer cell can instead slip out the bottom, into the tissue underneath. This is **basal extrusion**. It survives there because it has turned up its own survival signals and dodges apoptosis.

{{slide:L9-16|Normal cells extrude into the lumen and die; the tumor cell extrudes basally and survives.}}

:::hook
Normal cell falls **up** into the tube and dies. Cancer cell falls **down** into the tissue and lives.
:::

**The steps of metastasis:**

1. Let go of neighbors. The cell loses the adhesion proteins that hold it to the cells around it.
2. Break out. It cuts through the basal lamina.
3. Hop on. It squeezes into a blood vessel. This is called **intravasation** ("into the vessel").
4. Ride. It travels through the bloodstream. Fewer than 1 in 1000 cells survive this part.
5. Hop off. It sticks to a vessel wall in a far-away organ (like the liver) and squeezes out.
6. Settle in. It grows into a tiny tumor (a **micrometastasis**), then a full-size new tumor (a **metastasis**).

Very few cells make it all the way. But the ones that do cause about **90% of cancer deaths**.

"**Let go, Break out, Hop on, Ride, Hop off, Settle in.**" Six steps. And two numbers: **under 1 in 1000 survive the ride**, but metastasis causes **90% of deaths**.

:::exam
Her Q4: "Define metastasis. Explain the rationale for batimastat, an inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinases."

Definition, in one sentence: metastasis is the spread of cancer cells from the original (primary) tumor through the blood or lymph to distant sites, where they form new (secondary) tumors.

New term she expects you to reason through: **matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)** are enzymes that cut up the extracellular matrix, including the basal lamina. "Metallo" means they need a metal (zinc) to work, and "proteinase" means they cut proteins.

Rationale: to metastasize, a cell has to cut its way through the basal lamina (step 2, "break out") and through more matrix to get into and out of blood vessels (steps 3 and 5). Cancer cells use MMPs for that cutting. Block the MMPs and the cells can't invade, so they stay put. Batimastat isn't meant to kill tumor cells. It keeps them from leaving home, which is the step that causes about 90% of cancer deaths.

Think of MMPs as the cell's bolt cutters for the fence. Batimastat takes away the bolt cutters.
:::

:::conn
Lecture 11 has the body's own version of this guard. PAI-1, switched on by TGFβ, blocks the enzymes that chew up the matrix. Lose Smad4 and you lose PAI-1, so the matrix gets chewed and metastasis gets easier. Batimastat is the drug version of the same idea.
:::

### The tumor microenvironment

:::key
- A tumor contains normal helper cells as well as cancer cells
- The supporting tissue around the cancer cells is called the stroma, and it helps the tumor grow
:::

The short version: a tumor isn't made only of cancer cells. It pulls in normal neighbors that help it.

Alongside the cancer cells, a tumor contains ordinary cells and materials:

- **fibroblasts**, the cells that build connective tissue
- **endothelial cells** and **pericytes**, the cells that build blood vessels
- cells of the lymph vessels
- white blood cells
- extracellular matrix

The supporting connective tissue around the cancer cells is called the **stroma**. These neighbors change how the cancer grows, so a tumor works more like a small organ than a pile of identical cells.

### Angiogenesis

:::key
- Without its own blood vessels, a tumor can't grow past about 2 mm
- Tumors release signals (bFGF, TGFα, VEGF) that make new vessels grow toward them
- Two experiments (thyroid and eye) showed blood supply alone decides whether a tumor grows
:::

The short version: without its own blood supply, a tumor stalls at about 2 mm. So tumors send out signals that make blood vessels grow toward them.

**Angiogenesis** means growing new blood vessels ("angio" = vessel, "genesis" = making).

Why does a tumor need them? Food and oxygen can only seep a short distance through tissue. Without vessels running into it, a tumor can only reach about **2 mm** across. Past that, the cells in the middle starve and die. That messy, unplanned death is called **necrosis** (different from the tidy suicide of apoptosis).

To get past 2 mm, many tumors release growth factors that make vessels grow:

- **bFGF** (basic fibroblast growth factor)
- **TGFα** (transforming growth factor alpha)
- **VEGF** (vascular endothelial growth factor, "the grow-a-blood-vessel factor")

Here's what happens next. The cells lining a nearby tiny blood vessel (a **capillary**) push out a sprout. The sprout hollows out into a new tube and grows into the tumor. Now the tumor gets more blood, so it grows more, which means more cell divisions and more chances for new harmful mutations.

:::hook
A tumor is a city that needs roads. At 2 mm it runs out of delivery trucks, so it builds highways (vessels) by shouting VEGF. "**2 mm without a highway.**"
:::

:::she
"Two experiments illustrating the requirement for angiogenesis."
:::

1. **The thyroid experiment.** A thyroid gland was removed and kept alive by pumping nutrient liquid through it, so it had no working blood vessels. Cancer cells injected into it formed tumors that stopped growing at 1-2 mm.
2. **The eye experiment.** Tumor cells were put into the front chamber of the eye, which is filled with fluid and has no blood vessels. They stayed tiny. Tumor cells put on the **iris** (the colored part, which does have blood vessels) grew fast, and their size shot up within days.

Both experiments use the same tumor cells. The only difference is the blood supply, and the blood supply decides whether the tumor grows.

:::hook
"**Thyroid in a jar, eye with and without a road.**" Two setups, same answer: no blood, no growth.
:::

### Hypoxia, HIF and VEGF

:::key
- HIF is the cell's "we need oxygen" alarm. When oxygen is low, it switches on VEGF to call for blood vessels
- When oxygen is high, HIF gets an -OH tag, the VHL garbage crew grabs it, and the proteasome destroys it
- If VHL is broken, HIF is never destroyed, so VEGF stays on and vessel-packed tumors form (VHL syndrome)
:::

The short version: HIF is the "we need oxygen" alarm. Oxygen normally gets HIF thrown in the trash. When oxygen is low, HIF survives and calls for blood vessels.

The words you need:

- **Hypoxia** means low oxygen.
- **HIF** (hypoxia-inducible factor) is a transcription factor, a protein that switches genes on. One of its target genes is **VEGF**, the signal that makes blood vessels grow.
- **Ubiquitin** is a small protein tag. A chain of ubiquitin on a protein means "destroy me."
- An **E3 ubiquitin ligase** is the enzyme that sticks ubiquitin chains onto a protein.
- The **proteasome** is the cell's protein shredder. It destroys anything tagged with ubiquitin.

**When oxygen is plentiful,** HIF is destroyed as fast as it's made:

1. Using oxygen, the cell adds an -OH group onto certain prolines (one of the amino acids) in HIF. Adding an -OH is called **hydroxylation**.
2. That -OH tag lets an E3 ubiquitin ligase grab HIF and cover it in ubiquitin. **VHL** is one of the subunits of this E3 ligase.
3. The proteasome shreds HIF. This tag-and-shred route is called the **ubiquitin-proteasome pathway**.

**When oxygen is low,** step 1 can't happen. HIF gets no -OH tag, so the E3 ligase can't grab it. HIF builds up, switches on VEGF and other low-oxygen genes, and new blood vessels grow toward the area.

| | Normal oxygen | Low oxygen (hypoxia) |
|---|---|---|
| Does HIF get the -OH tag? | yes | no |
| Can the VHL garbage crew grab HIF? | yes | no |
| What happens to HIF? | it's destroyed | it builds up and switches on genes |
| VEGF | off | on, so new vessels grow |

:::she
Human Perspective: von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome. The VHL protein is a subunit of the E3 ubiquitin ligase that destroys HIF. When it's defective, cells make large amounts of HIF regardless of oxygen. The result is **hemangioblastomas**, tumors packed with dense masses of blood vessels.
:::

:::trap
In VHL syndrome, the cell isn't making more HIF. It just never destroys it. That's a broken OFF switch again, so the cell acts as if it's always short of oxygen and keeps making VEGF.
:::

:::hook
**HIF = "Help, I'm Fainting!"** It only shouts for blood vessels when oxygen is low. Oxygen puts the -OH "trash tag" on it; **VHL is the garbage collector**. Lose the garbage collector and the alarm never stops, so you get tumors full of blood vessels.
:::

:::nancy
For VHL syndrome she writes that cells "produce large quantities of HIF regardless of O2 levels" (and spells it "Hippel-Landau").

Plain version: HIF isn't being made faster. It just isn't being destroyed, because VHL (the garbage collector) is broken. The name is von Hippel-Lindau.
:::
