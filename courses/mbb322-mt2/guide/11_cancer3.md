## Lecture 11: cancer III, the brakes and the pathways they guard {#l11}

:::story
Chapter 7, the last one. Lecture 10 was about gas pedals: proto-oncogenes that get stuck ON (RasD, Bcr-Abl, an RTK that fires with no ligand). This chapter is the other half: brakes. Tumor suppressor genes stop cells from dividing, and cancer needs them broken. Then we meet two new signaling pathways, Wnt and TGFβ, because their parts are some of the most commonly broken brakes and pedals in colon cancer. The chapter ends by putting every cancer gene from Lectures 9 to 11 on one map.

What you need from before: the multi-hit model and the colon cancer steps (L9), proto-oncogene vs oncogene (L10), phosphorylation as a switch and ubiquitin as a "destroy me" tag (L5, L9), and 7-pass receptors (L5).
:::

### The big picture in one paragraph

You have two copies of every brake gene, so a cell only loses control when both copies break. Kids who inherit one broken copy of the Rb brake get eye tumors early and in both eyes, because every cell already has one strike. The second copy can be lost in many ways, including being silenced with methyl tags while the DNA letters stay perfect. Then two pathways: Wnt, where a "destruction gang" keeps destroying a gene-switching protein (β-catenin) until Wnt arrives, and APC is a member of that gang; and TGFβ, a "stop dividing" signal that travels by Smad proteins, which colon cancers lose by deleting Smad4. TGFβ is strange because it's a brake early in cancer and helps the tumor late. The lecture closes with the colon cancer gene table and the three-class comparison.

:::hook
Lecture 11 in four words: "**Two hits, Wnt, TGFβ, map.**"
:::

### Part 1: brakes vs gas pedals

:::key
- A tumor suppressor is a brake gene. Cancer needs it broken (loss of function), and BOTH copies have to go
- An oncogene is a stuck gas pedal (gain of function). ONE bad copy is enough
:::

The short version: gas pedals need one bad copy. Brakes need both copies broken.

A **tumor suppressor gene** is a normal gene whose protein helps prevent cancer, usually by slowing division, triggering repair, or killing damaged cells. Cancer comes from a **loss-of-function** mutation: the brake stops working.

Compare that with Lecture 10. A proto-oncogene becomes an oncogene with a gain-of-function change, and **one** mutated copy is enough, because one stuck gas pedal drives the car. A tumor suppressor is different: you have two copies (one from each parent), and as long as one works, the brake still holds. You have to lose **both**.

{{fig:twohit}}

| | Oncogene (gas pedal) | Tumor suppressor (brake) |
|---|---|---|
| What the mutation does | the protein does more than it should (gain of function) | the protein stops working (loss of function) |
| How many of the two copies must break | one is enough | both |
| Inside one cell, it behaves as | dominant (one copy shows its effect) | recessive (only shows once both copies are gone) |

:::hook
Gas pedal: one bad copy is enough (dominant at the cell level). Brakes: you have two brake lines, and the car only crashes when both are cut (recessive at the cell level).
:::

### Part 2: retinoblastoma and the two-hit idea

:::key
- Rb, on chromosome 13, was the first tumor suppressor found. It's a brake on the cell cycle
- Kids who inherit one broken Rb copy get many tumors, in both eyes
- Kids born with two good copies need two hits in the same cell, so it's rare (about 1 in 30,000) and gives one tumor in one eye
- It runs in families like a dominant trait, but inside each cell it's recessive
:::

The short version: kids born with one broken Rb copy get many tumors in both eyes; kids born with two good copies rarely get one tumor. That difference proved you need two hits.

The first tumor suppressor gene was found by studying **retinoblastoma**, a rare childhood cancer of the retina (the light-sensing layer at the back of the eye). Some patients had a small deletion on **chromosome 13**, which pointed researchers to the gene, now called **Rb**.

The Rb protein puts the brakes on the **cell cycle** (the series of steps a cell goes through to divide). Without Rb, cells start dividing when they shouldn't. Rb loss also shows up in many other cancers.

Retinoblastoma comes in two forms, and comparing them is what proved the two-hit idea:

| | Hereditary retinoblastoma | Sporadic (non-hereditary) retinoblastoma |
|---|---|---|
| What the child starts with | one broken Rb copy, already in **every** cell of the body | two good Rb copies |
| What has to go wrong to get a tumor | **one** more hit, in any one of the millions of retina cells | **two** separate hits, both landing in the **same** cell |
| What her slide says happens | most get several tumors, in **both** eyes | it's rare (about **1 in 30,000** people), and they get **one** tumor in **one** eye |

Why the difference? Every retina cell in the hereditary child already has hit number one. With millions of cells, a second hit somewhere is almost certain, and it happens in several places. In the sporadic case, two rare events have to land in the same cell, which almost never happens, so it's one tumor at most.

{{slide:L11-2|Middle column: one inherited hit means many cells need only one more, so tumors in both eyes. Right column: two hits in one cell is rare, so one tumor.}}

Rb is also turned off in many common adult cancers. Those show up later in life because they need a longer series of genetic changes.

:::trap
Family pattern vs cell behavior. The hereditary form runs in families like a dominant trait (inherit one bad copy and you'll very likely get tumors). But inside each cell, Rb is recessive (both copies must be lost before the cell loses control). If a question asks whether a tumor suppressor mutation is "dominant or recessive," check whether it means the family pattern or the cell. Her table (Table 25-1) says tumor suppressor mutations are genetically recessive.
:::

:::hook
**Knudson's two hits** in baseball: the hereditary kid starts every at-bat with one strike already called, in every cell. Sporadic kids need two strikes in the same at-bat. "**Rb = Retina brake, chromosome 13**" (unlucky 13).
:::

### Part 3: how the second copy gets lost

:::key
- The second hit doesn't have to be a new mutation in the good copy
- There are six ways to lose it: lose the chromosome, swap chromosome arms, copy the bad version over the good one, cut it out, change one letter, or silence it with methyl tags
:::

The short version: there are six ways to lose the good copy, and only some of them are a new mutation.

Hit two doesn't have to be a fresh mutation in the remaining gene. Her figure (Fig. 20-25) shows six ways a cell with one good Rb copy can lose it:

| # | Name | What happens to the good copy |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Aneuploidy** | The whole chromosome carrying it is lost when the cell divides. (Aneuploidy means having the wrong number of chromosomes.) |
| 2 | **Mitotic recombination** | While the cell divides, the two chromosomes swap arms. One daughter cell ends up with two bad copies and no good one. |
| 3 | **Gene conversion** | During that swapping, the good copy gets overwritten to match the bad one. |
| 4 | **Deletion** | The piece of DNA holding it is cut out. |
| 5 | **Point mutation** | One DNA letter changes and breaks it. |
| 6 | **Epigenetic silencing** | It's still there with perfect letters, but chemical tags switch it off. |

{{slide:L11-3|Six columns, one result each time: no working Rb left.}}

:::hook
"**Lose, Swap, Copy, Cut, Typo, Muzzle**": lose the chromosome, swap arms, copy the bad version over, cut it out, typo it, or muzzle it with methyl groups.
:::

#### Epigenetic silencing

:::key
- Epigenetic means a change in which genes are on that gets passed to daughter cells, with NO change to the DNA letters
- Tightly packed DNA (heterochromatin) keeps genes off
- Enzymes called DNMTs stick methyl tags on the C's in a gene's on-switch (CpG islands), which turns the gene off
- A silenced brake gene still counts as a hit
:::

The short version: the gene's letters are perfect, but chemical tags stop the cell from reading it, and those tags get passed on.

**Epigenetics** means a change in which genes are switched on that gets passed to daughter cells but does **not** change the DNA letters. ("Epi" means "on top of": the change sits on top of the genes.) Cancer cells do this two ways:

- Heterochromatin. DNA in the nucleus is wrapped around proteins called histones; together they're called chromatin. **Heterochromatin** is the tightly packed form that keeps genes silent, like a rolled-up, rubber-banded map you can't read. It forms when certain chemical marks on histones are bound by proteins that pack chromatin. Some cancer cells have an unusually large amount of it, so more genes get silenced.
- DNA methylation of CpG islands. In front of many genes is a **promoter**, the "on switch" region. Many promoters have stretches rich in C followed by G, called **CpG islands**. In an active promoter, those Cs are bare (unmethylated). Enzymes called **DNA methyltransferases** take a methyl group (-CH3) from a donor molecule called **SAM** and stick it onto carbon 5 of the cytosine ring, making **5-methylcytosine (5mC)**. That makes the chromatin pack up and shuts the gene off.

{{fig:methylation}}

:::trap
Epigenetic silencing still counts as a "hit" and still causes loss of function, even though the letters are perfect. A question might describe a tumor where "the Rb gene sequence is normal but no Rb protein is made." That's methylation of the promoter, and it's still loss of the tumor suppressor.
:::

:::hook
Methyl groups are **tape over the gene's on-switch**. The switch is fine; you just can't press it. "**SAM** hands over the **M**ethyl." CpG = "**C**, then **G**," like "Cat, then Gate": tape the gate shut.
:::

:::conn
This links back to Lecture 9: mutant IDH made 2-hydroxyglutarate, which jammed enzymes that control gene expression (including ones that remove methyl tags). Metabolism can push a cell toward this kind of silencing.
:::

### Part 4: spotting a gas pedal vs a brake from the mutations alone

:::key
- Oncogene: missense hotspots (e.g. Ras Gly12)
- Tumor suppressor: scattered, often truncating
:::

The short version: gas pedal mutations pile up on the same exact spot. Brake mutations are scattered everywhere and often chop the protein short.

Her Fig. 20-26 compares where mutations land in an oncogene versus a tumor suppressor gene across many patients' tumors.

| | Oncogene | Tumor suppressor |
|---|---|---|
| Where mutations land | a few exact **hotspots**, often in the kinase part | **spread along the whole gene** |
| Kind of mutation | **missense** (one amino acid swapped) | many are **truncating** (an early stop signal, so the protein comes out chopped short) |
| Why | to make a protein overactive you have to hit one of the few spots that controls it (Ras Gly 12 is the classic) | there are endless ways to break something |

{{fig:mutpattern}}

:::hook
It's easier to break a watch than to make it run fast. Breaking (tumor suppressor) can happen anywhere; making it run fast (oncogene) needs one specific screw turned. "**Pile-up = pedal. Scatter = smashed brake.**"
:::

:::trap
If a data figure shows mutations clustered at the same one or two amino acids in many patients, she wants "oncogene." Scattered truncating mutations means "tumor suppressor." This is a reasoning question, so look at the pattern before reading the options.
:::

### Part 5: the five jobs of tumor suppressor proteins

:::key
- Five classes: growth-inhibiting signals (TGFβ), cell cycle (Rb), checkpoint (p53), apoptosis, DNA repair (BRCA1/2)
- Caretaker loss speeds up all other mutations
:::

The short version: five kinds of brakes, from blocking the "go" signal to fixing DNA.

Her slide lists five classes:

| Class | What the protein does | Example from this unit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | part of a signaling pathway that holds back growth | TGFβ pathway (Part 7 below) |
| 2 | holds back progress through the cell cycle | Rb |
| 3 | checkpoint: stops the cell cycle when DNA is damaged | p53 |
| 4 | pushes damaged cells into apoptosis (programmed death) | (pro-apoptotic proteins) |
| 5 | DNA repair enzymes (**caretaker** genes) | BRCA1 and BRCA2, which repair breaks through both DNA strands |

Losing a class 5 (caretaker) protein doesn't directly make cells divide. It lets mutations build up everywhere, including in genes that control growth. That's the genetic instability from Lecture 9.

**BRCA1** helps repair DNA damage, including damage from radiation. Inherited BRCA1 mutations raise breast cancer risk.

:::hook
"**Signal, Cycle, Check, Kill, Fix**": stop the go signal (TGFβ), hold the cycle (Rb), checkpoint (p53), kill the cell (apoptosis), fix the DNA (BRCA). p53 is the **airport security checkpoint**: damaged DNA doesn't board.
:::

:::nancy
She writes "Five main classes of proteins encoded by tumor suppressor proteins."

Plain version: she means tumor suppressor GENES. Proteins don't encode anything; genes do.
:::

### Part 6: the Wnt pathway, where APC lives

#### Why we care

:::key
- APC: Wnt pathway tumor suppressor, mutated in >80% of colon cancers; FAP = inherited polyps
:::

**APC** (adenomatous polyposis coli) is a tumor suppressor broken in **more than 80% of colon cancers**. Its role was found through families with **familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)**, an inherited condition where the colon fills with hundreds of polyps (small growths). APC is part of the **Wnt signaling pathway**, so to understand APC you need Wnt.

{{slide:L11-6|Normal colon lining on top, a colon carpeted in polyps below. That's what inheriting one bad APC copy does.}}

:::hook
"**FAP = Full of Adenomatous Polyps.**" When she writes FAP, she means APC.
:::

#### How Wnt was discovered (three animals, one gene)

:::key
- Wnt = wingless (fly) + Int1 (mouse mammary tumors)
- Wnt1 RNA in ventral Xenopus cells → duplicated body axis

*Skim:* Which labs and model systems: skim.
:::

The short version: a mouse tumor gene and a fly wing gene turned out to be the same gene, and frogs proved what it does.

| Animal | What they found |
|---|---|
| Mouse | switching on the first mouse Wnt gene, **Int1**, in the wrong place (**ectopic** activation) caused breast (mammary) tumors. Its sequence showed a cysteine-rich protein that gets released from the cell. |
| Fruit fly | the gene **wingless (wg)** turned out to be the same gene. It sets the front-back pattern inside each body segment of the embryo (**segment polarity**) and is needed to make wings. **Wingless + Int = Wnt.** |
| Frog | injecting mouse Wnt1 RNA into the belly-side (**ventral**) cells of a 4-cell *Xenopus* embryo made a tadpole with a **duplicated body axis**: two body plans on one tadpole. |

Her slide credits figuring out the mechanism to genetics in flies and worms, biochemistry in cultured cells, and the out-of-place expression in *Xenopus*.

:::hook
**Wnt = Wingless + Int.** Fly wings, mouse tumors, frogs with two bodies: same gene.
:::

#### The pathway: think "destruction gang"

:::key
- No Wnt: axin/APC/GSK-3/CK1 phosphorylate β-catenin → ubiquitin → proteasome
- Wnt: Frizzled + LRP → Dishevelled pulls axin → complex falls apart
- β-catenin → nucleus, kicks off Groucho, binds TCF → Myc ON
- APC = tumor suppressor, β-catenin = oncogene
:::

The short version: without Wnt, a gang keeps destroying β-catenin. Wnt breaks up the gang, β-catenin survives, walks into the nucleus and switches on growth genes.

The star is **β-catenin**, a **transcriptional co-activator** (a helper that lets a gene-switching protein turn genes on). The whole pathway is about one question: does β-catenin survive?

**Without Wnt (pathway OFF):**

1. A **destruction complex** grabs β-catenin. It's made of **axin** (the scaffold that holds it together), **APC**, and two kinases: **GSK-3** (glycogen synthase kinase-3) and **CK1** (casein kinase 1).
2. GSK-3 and CK1 phosphorylate β-catenin.
3. The phosphorylated β-catenin gets tagged with **ubiquitin** and shredded by the **proteasome**.
4. In the nucleus, the gene-switching protein **TCF** (also called **LEF**) sits on the Wnt target genes with a blocker called **Groucho**, keeping them OFF.

**With Wnt (pathway ON):**

1. Wnt binds **Frizzled**, a seven-pass receptor, together with a helper receptor (co-receptor) called **LRP**.
2. A protein called **Dishevelled** joins Frizzled and pulls axin away.
3. The destruction complex falls apart. β-catenin isn't phosphorylated or destroyed anymore, so it builds up.
4. β-catenin enters the nucleus, kicks Groucho off, and joins TCF. Wnt target genes switch ON. One of them is **Myc**, the growth-promoting transcription factor from the myc + rasD mouse experiment in Lecture 9.

| | No Wnt | Wnt present |
|---|---|---|
| Destruction complex (axin, APC, GSK-3, CK1) | assembled, working | broken up by Dishevelled |
| β-catenin | phosphorylated, destroyed | safe, builds up |
| In the nucleus | TCF + Groucho, genes OFF | TCF + β-catenin, genes ON (Myc) |

{{fig:wnt}}

{{lab:wnt|Wnt / β-catenin (APC loss, stabilized β-catenin, GSK-3)}}

{{slide:L11-8|Without Wnt: the destruction complex (axin, APC, GSK-3, CK1) phosphorylates β-catenin, and Groucho keeps TCF's genes off.}}

{{slide:L11-9|With Wnt: Dishevelled pulls axin away, β-catenin escapes, enters the nucleus and kicks Groucho off TCF.}}

:::hook
β-catenin is a messenger who keeps getting mugged on the way to the nucleus. The destruction complex is the gang: axin is the getaway car, APC is a member, GSK-3 and CK1 tag him with phosphates. Wnt breaks up the gang (Dishevelled "**dishevels**" it, messes it up), and the messenger finally delivers "turn on Myc." **Groucho is the grouch** sitting on the genes until β-catenin shoves him off.
:::

:::easy
Signal-in-reverse trap: in Wnt, phosphorylation of β-catenin means DESTRUCTION (pathway off). Wnt works by STOPPING phosphorylation. If you assume "phosphorylated = active" you'll get this backwards. Same logic as HIF in Lecture 9: tagged in good conditions, destroyed.
:::

:::easy
Name trap: the TCF in Wnt (T-cell factor, also LEF) is a different transcription factor from the TCF in Lecture 8 (ternary complex factor, which sits with SRF on the c-fos SRE). Same three letters, different pathways.
:::

:::exam
Her Q21: how does β-catenin turn genes on once it's in the nucleus? It binds TCF/LEF, displaces the co-repressor Groucho, and recruits co-activators. β-catenin isn't a kinase and doesn't bind DNA itself. It works by changing which partner sits on TCF.

Her distractors pull from other pathways. STAT belongs to JAK-STAT (Lecture 10), SMADs belong to TGFβ, and APC works in the cytoplasm as part of the destruction complex.

Her practice question calls Groucho a "co-repressor," which settles the "co-receptor" wording on her slide.
:::

:::easy
Both-boxes trap: APC is a tumor suppressor, but β-catenin (in the same pathway) is an oncogene. Losing APC and over-activating β-catenin do the same thing: β-catenin stays high. In her colon table (Table 20-1) they share a footnote because a tumor usually has one or the other, rarely both.
:::

:::trap
Frizzled crosses the membrane seven times like a GPCR, but on her slides the Wnt signal goes through Dishevelled and the destruction complex, not through the Gα / second messenger chain from Lecture 5. If asked how Wnt reaches the nucleus, answer with β-catenin surviving, not cAMP.
:::

:::conn
GSK-3 stands for glycogen synthase kinase-3. The name comes from sugar storage (the Lecture 5 story where PKA shut off glycogen synthase). The same kinase family shows up here doing a completely different job: the "same signal, different use" thread again.
:::

:::nancy
Two lines on the Wnt slides read harder than they are. She writes that β-catenin "displaces the co-receptor Groucho," and that Dishevelled "recruits axin. This leads to the disassembly of the destruction complex."

Plain version: Groucho is a co-REPRESSOR (it keeps genes off), not a co-receptor; if she writes co-receptor she still means Groucho. And "recruits axin" sounds like Dishevelled builds the gang, but pulling axin away is what breaks the gang apart.
:::

#### Wnts carry a fat tag

:::key
- Palmitate on serine binds Frizzled
- 19 human Wnts

*Skim:* Dally / Dlps and the disease slide: recognize.
:::

The short version: Wnts have a fatty acid stuck on them that helps them grab Frizzled and controls how far they travel.

- Humans have **19 Wnts**. They're essential for brain development, patterning limbs, forming organs and building bone, and they drive division of many cell types.
- Wnts carry a **palmitate** (a fatty acid) attached to a **serine**. The palmitate grips Frizzled and holds the Wnt-Frizzled pair together.
- In flies, **Dally** and Dally-like proteins (**Dlps**) grab that fat and control how far Wnts spread. Her table lists Dally as a **heparan sulfate proteoglycan** (a protein with long sugar chains, on the cell surface) that works as a co-receptor for Wingless and **Dpp**.

:::hook
"**Palmitate = Wnt's Palm grip on Frizzled.**" 19 Wnts.
:::

:::conn
Two Midterm 1 ideas come back here. Palmitoylation was one of the lipid anchors on MT1 question 2. Proteoglycans with heparan sulfate (Dally) were MT1 extracellular matrix material, and heparan sulfate also helped FGF bind its receptor in Lecture 8.
:::

Her disease slide shows Wnt pathway parts tied to many conditions: cancers (colorectal, liver), bone disorders (osteoporosis, osteoarthritis), and neurological, metabolic and heart and blood vessel diseases.

### Part 7: the TGFβ pathway, a brake that can turn into a pedal

#### The family

:::key
- 33 members: TGFβ, activins/inhibins, BMPs, GDFs
- Activin activates FSH, inhibin inhibits it, BMPs build bone

*Skim:* Discovery details for each branch: skim.
:::

The short version: a big family of related signals; she wants you to know four branches and what each was first found doing.

The **TGFβ superfamily** has **33 members** in humans, named after the first one found, TGFβ. Branches on her slides:

| Branch | First found as... | Other jobs |
|---|---|---|
| **TGFβ** (TGFβ1, 2, 3) | (see the discovery paradox below) | |
| **Activin** | a protein that makes the pituitary make and release **FSH** (follicle-stimulating hormone, which controls development, growth and puberty); also makes mesoderm in frog embryos | many roles from embryo to adult |
| **Inhibin** | a hormone that **blocks** FSH production | many roles from embryo to adult |
| **BMPs** (bone morphogenetic proteins) | extracts of demineralized bone that, mixed with collagen and injected under a rat's skin, made **new bone** | set the back-belly (dorsal-ventral) axis in flies; organ development; making neurons |
| **GDFs** (growth differentiation factors) | | many roles in development |

:::hook
"**Activin Activates FSH, Inhibin Inhibits it, BMPs Build Bone.**"
:::

#### The discovery paradox

:::key
- Named for transforming fibroblasts, but mostly SUPPRESSES growth in epithelial/lymphoid cells
- Dual role: tumor suppressor early, pro-oncogenic late
:::

The short version: it was named for making cells cancerous, then turned out to mostly stop cells growing.

TGFβ was found in the late 1970s and early 1980s as a factor that could **transform** normal fibroblasts into cells that grew aggressively in a dish. That's where the name "transforming growth factor" comes from, and it suggested TGFβ promotes cancer.

Then it turned out TGFβ is made all over normal tissues and **blocks growth** of many human cells, including epithelial cells and lymphoid (immune) cells, the cell types that give rise to most human cancers.

So TGFβ has a **dual role**:

| | Early (normal cells, early tumors) | Late (advanced cancer) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | **tumor suppressor** | **pro-oncogenic** (helps the tumor) |
| What it does | blocks growth, triggers apoptosis, keeps the genome stable | **EMT** (epithelial cells becoming loose, mobile cells), invasion, survival, plus effects on the surroundings like suppressing the immune system and growing blood vessels |

In the Roberts and Wakefield figure, as tumors progress, cells stop responding to TGFβ's "stop" message while TGFβ levels go up.

{{fig:tgfdual}}

:::hook
TGFβ is a strict teacher. In a normal class, the teacher keeps everyone in their seats (brake). Once the class stops listening, the same teacher's yelling just makes the room more chaotic (helps the tumor). "**Brake early, booster late.**"
:::

:::easy
Both-boxes trap: TGFβ is a tumor suppressor early AND pro-oncogenic late. And its name says "transforming," but in normal epithelial cells it blocks growth. If a question asks "Is TGFβ a tumor suppressor or an oncogene?", the answer is "it depends on the stage," followed by the reason.
:::

:::nancy
She writes that in later stages TGFβ "functions as a proto-oncogene and promotes oncogenesis."

Plain version: she means it acts like an oncogene (it helps the cancer). Strictly, a proto-oncogene is the normal, healthy version of a gene, so the word is used loosely here. Also, "may other family members prevent proliferation" on that slide is a typo for "many."
:::

#### How the signal travels: receptor to Smad to DNA

:::key
- Type I and II receptors are Ser/Thr kinases
- Type II phosphorylates type I
- Type I phosphorylates R-Smad (Smad2/3) → joins Smad4 → nucleus
:::

The short version: receptor II switches on receptor I, receptor I tags an R-Smad, the R-Smad grabs Smad4, and they go into the nucleus.

1. There are two receptor kinds, **type I** and **type II**. Both are **serine/threonine kinases** (they tag serine and threonine, not tyrosine), and each works as a pair of identical pieces (a homodimer).
2. The TGFβ ligand is itself a pair. It binds the receptors and brings type I and type II together.
3. The **type II receptor phosphorylates and switches on the type I receptor**.
4. The active type I receptor grabs and phosphorylates an **R-Smad** (receptor-regulated Smad: **Smad2 or Smad3**).
5. The phosphorylated R-Smad lets go of the receptor and joins **Smad4**, the **co-Smad** ("partner Smad").
6. The group enters the nucleus and controls which genes are read.

{{fig:smad}}

{{lab:tgf|TGFβ / Smads (Smad4 loss, type II receptor loss)}}

{{slide:L11-16|Her version: type II receptor phosphorylates type I, which phosphorylates Smad2/3; Smad4 joins and they head for the nucleus.}}

:::hook
"**Two tags One**": type II switches on type I, never the reverse (the older sibling turns on the younger). Then "**Receptor II, Receptor I, R-Smad, Smad4, nucleus.**" The **R**-Smad is the one the **R**eceptor touches; **Smad4 is the "4-ever partner"** that every R-Smad pairs with. "**Smad 2 and 3 get tagged, Smad 4 tags along.**"
:::

:::easy
Category trap: TGFβ receptors are enzyme-coupled receptors with their own kinase domains, like RTKs, but they phosphorylate serine and threonine, not tyrosine. "Receptor kinase" does not automatically mean "receptor tyrosine kinase."

|---|---|---|
| Kinase inside? | yes | yes |
| Tags which amino acid | tyrosine | serine / threonine |
| How it switches on | two receptors tag each other | type II tags type I |
| Messenger to nucleus | Ras → Raf → MEK → ERK | Smad2/3 + Smad4 |

:::nancy
She writes that the Smad complex "translocates to the nucleus."

Plain version: "translocates" here just means moves into the nucleus. It's not a chromosome swap like the Philadelphia translocation in Lecture 9.

#### What phosphorylation of a Smad actually does

:::key
- Phosphorylation unmasks the NLS (in MH1) → importin carries it in
- In the nucleus: binds DNA with a partner (TFE3) to regulate genes

*Skim:* Exportins and nuclear phosphatase: recognize.
:::

:::she
"What does phosphorylation of Smads do? How do Smads regulate transcription?" (written as questions on her slide)
:::

The short version: the phosphate uncovers the Smad's "deliver me to the nucleus" label.

- R-Smads have two parts: **MH1**, which binds DNA and holds an **NLS** (nuclear localization signal: the "zip code" that gets a protein carried into the nucleus), and **MH2**.
- Before phosphorylation, the NLS is hidden. **Phosphorylation causes a shape change that uncovers the NLS.**
- Two phosphorylated R-Smads join Smad4 and an **importin** (the nuclear import carrier), and move into the nucleus.
- In the nucleus, the group binds DNA and teams up with another transcription factor (**TFE3** in her figure) to switch on target genes.
- R-Smads get changed again in the nucleus (her figure shows a phosphatase taking the phosphate off), which controls their activity and their trip back out through **exportins**.

:::hook
Phosphorylation peels the sticker off the Smad's address label. Once the NLS (zip code) shows, importin delivers it to the nucleus; exportin is the return mail.
:::

:::conn
This is the third way this unit uses phosphorylation. In Lectures 5 and 8, phosphates switch enzymes on or off or make SH2 landing pads. In Wnt, a phosphate marks β-catenin for destruction. Here, a phosphate uncovers a hidden address label. Ask "what does THIS phosphate do?" every time.
:::

| Where | What the phosphate does |
|---|---|
| PKA on glycogen synthase (L5) | turns it off |
| RTK tails (L8) | makes landing pads for SH2 |
| Src Tyr 527 (L10) | locks Src off |
| β-catenin (L11) | marks it for destruction |
| R-Smad (L11) | uncovers the NLS so it enters the nucleus |

#### Where the name Smad comes from

:::key
- Smad = Sma (worm) + Mad (fly, found in a dpp enhancer screen)
:::

The fly gene **decapentaplegic (dpp)** is a TGFβ family member that patterns the back-belly axis and the **wing imaginal discs** (the clusters of cells in the larva that become wings). A screen for **enhancers** of weak *dpp* mutants found **Mad** (mothers against dpp) as a part needed downstream. At the same time, worm researchers found the **sma** genes in *C. elegans*. **Mad + Sma = Smad.**

:::hook
"**Smad = Sma (worm) + Mad (fly).**" Like Wnt = Wingless + Int, it's another name glued together from two animals.
:::

:::conn
Same strategy as the Sevenless enhancer and suppressor screens in Lecture 8: start with a weakened pathway, then look for mutations that make it worse or better to find the downstream parts.
:::

#### When TGFβ signaling is lost, cells escape the brake

:::key
- Smad2/Smad4 on 18q; Smad4 = DPC
- Loss → no p15 (keeps dividing) and less PAI-1 (more matrix breakdown, metastasis)
- Same as the L9 chromosome 18 step
:::

The short version: lose Smad4 or a receptor, and TGFβ can't tell cells to stop, and the matrix around them gets chewed up more easily.

- Smad2 and Smad4 both sit on **chromosome 18q**, which is often mutated or lost in pancreatic and colon cancers. Smad4 was first called **DPC** ("deleted in pancreatic cancer").
- Without Smad signaling, cells can't make **p15** and other cell-cycle blockers in response to TGFβ, so TGFβ can no longer stop them dividing.
- Other cancers lose the type I or type II receptors instead.
- Her figure also shows that losing the pathway lowers **PAI-1**, a protein that blocks enzymes that chew up the extracellular matrix. Less PAI-1 means more matrix breakdown, which helps metastasis.

{{slide:L11-18|Two places the brake can break: the receptors (top) or Smad4 (middle). Result: no p15 (more division) and no PAI-1 (more ECM breakdown, metastasis).}}

:::hook
Lose the pathway and lose **two P's**: **p15** (the "stop dividing" order) and **PAI-1** (the "don't chew the matrix" guard). "**DPC = Deleted in Pancreatic Cancer = Smad4, on 18q.**"
:::

:::conn
The "chromosome 18 loss" step in the Lecture 9 colon sequence, labeled "DCC region" on that older figure, is where Smad4 and Smad2 live. Her updated colon sequence in this lecture calls that step "loss of Smad4 and other tumor suppressors." Same step, now with a mechanism: the colon cells lose the TGFβ brake.
:::

#### FOP: when a BMP receptor won't turn off

:::key
- ACVR1 (BMP type I receptor) R206H in the GS domain
- Leaky signaling without ligand → bone in muscle
- Gain-of-function, dominant, NOT cancer

*Skim:* 1 in 2,000,000 and the bone disease table: skim.
:::

The short version: a single change in a BMP receptor makes it signal with no BMP, and muscle slowly turns into bone.

**Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP)** is a rare genetic disease (about **1 in 2,000,000** worldwide) where muscle and connective tissue slowly turn into bone, building a "second skeleton." Bone forming outside the skeleton is called **heterotopic ossification** ("bone in the wrong place"). Most cases are new (spontaneous); when it's inherited, it's **autosomal dominant**. The two classic signs are bone forming in the wrong places over time and malformed big toes.

The cause is a repeated mutation in **ACVR1**, a **BMP type I receptor**: a highly conserved **arginine at position 206** in the receptor's **GS domain** (a control region of the receptor) is changed to **histidine (R206H)**. All known FOP mutations in ACVR1 cause **leaky BMP signaling even with no ligand**.

{{slide:L11-23|Every FOP mutation sits in the GS or kinase domain of ACVR1, and all of them leak signal with no BMP bound.}}

:::easy
Same pattern as the Lecture 10 gain-of-function receptors (neu, erbB, the skier's Epo receptor): a receptor that signals without its ligand. FOP is gain-of-function and autosomal dominant, but it causes bone, not cancer. Gain-of-function doesn't automatically mean tumor.
:::

:::hook
**FOP: "Forms Out-of-Place bone."** **R206H: "Receptor 206 Hot-wired."** And "R to H" reads like "**R**eady to **H**arden."
:::

Many other inherited bone diseases come from mutations in TGFβ and BMP signaling genes (her Table 2 lists BMPR1B, GDF5, Smad4, Noggin and others), because these pathways build the skeleton.

### Part 8: putting it all together

#### Drivers vs passengers

:::key
- Drivers = genes repeatedly mutated across tumors (~1% of genes)
- Peaks: APC, K-Ras, p53, PIK3CA, FBXW7
:::

The short version: the genes that matter are the ones hit over and over in many tumors.

Besides big chromosome changes, tumors carry lots of single-letter mutations. Most are **passengers** (along for the ride, doing nothing). The ones that cause the cancer are **drivers**. How do you tell? Look for genes that are **mutated again and again** across many tumors and many cancer types. By that test, about **1% of genes** are thought to be cancer-critical. Her colorectal mutation map (titled "mutational landscape") shows a few tall peaks (APC, K-Ras, p53, PIK3CA, FBXW7) among many small hills.

{{slide:L11-24|The tall peaks are genes hit in many patients (drivers); the small bumps are mostly passengers.}}

:::hook
Drivers are the mountains on the map: the same genes, hit again and again. Passengers are the random little hills. Only about **1%** of genes are mountains.
:::

#### A handful of pathways

:::key
- Rb, RTK/Ras/PI3K, p53 pathways keep getting hit
:::

Different cancers break the same few pathways. In glioblastoma (a brain cancer) and ovarian cancer, three groups are commonly hit:

- the **Rb pathway** (starting cell division)
- the **RTK / Ras / PI3K pathway** (signals for growth and division)
- the **p53 pathway** (responses to stress and DNA damage)

:::hook
"**Start, Grow, Stress**": Rb (start dividing), RTK/Ras/PI3K (grow), p53 (stress check).
:::

#### The three classes, side by side (Table 25-1)

:::key
- Proto-oncogene: gain, dominant; point mutation/translocation/amplification
- Tumor suppressor and caretaker: loss, recessive; deletion/point mutation/methylation
:::

| | Proto-oncogenes | Tumor suppressor genes | Caretaker genes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal job | promote cell survival or division | hold back cell survival or division | repair or prevent DNA damage |
| Examples | anti-apoptotic proteins, signaling proteins, transcription factors | pro-apoptotic proteins, cell cycle blockers, checkpoint proteins, growth-restraining signals | DNA repair enzymes |
| Effect of mutation | gain-of-function | loss-of-function | loss-of-function, so mutations pile up |
| Genetics | dominant | recessive | recessive |
| How they arise | point mutation, translocation, amplification | deletion, point mutation, methylation | deletion, point mutation, methylation |

{{slide:L11-27|Read across each row: gas pedal (dominant, gain), brake (recessive, loss), mechanic (recessive, loss).}}

:::easy
Notice "methylation" appears for tumor suppressors and caretakers but not for proto-oncogenes. Silencing can only break something; it can't make a gas pedal stick. And "amplification" and "translocation" appear only for proto-oncogenes.
:::

:::hook
"**You can't tape a pedal down by covering it.**" Methylation only silences, so it only breaks brakes and mechanics. Amplification and translocation make more or new protein, so they only make pedals.
:::

:::exam
Her Q18: "Which fact about tumor-suppressor mutations is INCORRECT?" The false one is "they commonly arise through chromosomal translocation or gene amplification." Those are gas-pedal (oncogene) mechanisms, since they make more or new protein. Tumor suppressors are lost by deletion, point mutation or methylation.

The true options are worth reading as a summary. Tumor suppressors are brakes, both copies have to go before you see a phenotype, losing them allows unregulated growth and survival, and some of them promote apoptosis (like p53).

For any "which is INCORRECT" question, mark each option T or F before choosing. These questions have four true statements, so the false one is often the one that belongs to the opposite category.
:::

### How to figure it out: oncogene or tumor suppressor from data {#howto-ts}

How to spot it: a question gives you mutation data, family data, or a one-copy vs two-copy experiment, and asks what kind of gene it is.

Steps:

1. Where are the mutations? Same hotspot again and again → oncogene. Scattered, many truncating → tumor suppressor.
2. How many copies are hit in the tumor? One → oncogene. Both → tumor suppressor (or caretaker).
3. What does adding the normal gene back do? If putting a working copy into the tumor cell stops it growing, the tumor was missing a brake → tumor suppressor. Adding a normal copy doesn't fix an oncogene, because the stuck pedal is still there.
4. Sequence normal but no protein? Look for promoter methylation → tumor suppressor silenced epigenetically.
5. Family gets many tumors early, in both organs of a pair? Inherited first hit of a tumor suppressor.
6. Does the mutant make cells grow faster, or just pile up other mutations faster? The second one → caretaker.

:::hook
"**Add the good copy back: if it fixes it, a brake was missing. If it doesn't, a pedal is stuck.**"
:::

Practice:

- A gene is mutated in 40% of tumors, and almost every mutation changes codon 12 or 13. Oncogene (this is K-Ras).
- A gene shows nonsense and frameshift mutations scattered from start to end, and tumors have lost both copies. Tumor suppressor.
- A child has tumors in both eyes at age 2; a parent had retinoblastoma. Hereditary retinoblastoma: inherited one bad Rb copy, second hit in many retina cells.
- A tumor's MLH1 sequence is normal, but its promoter CpG island is heavily methylated and no MLH1 is made. A DNA repair (caretaker-type) gene silenced epigenetically; her colon table files it as a tumor suppressor for genetic stability. Expect many mutations elsewhere.
- Colon cells with normal APC but a β-catenin that GSK-3 can't phosphorylate. β-catenin is never destroyed, so Wnt target genes like Myc stay on. β-catenin is acting as an oncogene.
- Cells lacking Smad4 are treated with TGFβ. No p15 is made, so they keep dividing. Smad4 is a tumor suppressor.
- A patient's ACVR1 receptor signals with no BMP present. Gain-of-function receptor (FOP), a dominant mutation.
