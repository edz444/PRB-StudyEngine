# ---------- L5 GPCRs ----------
A GPCR crosses the plasma membrane {{c1::seven}} times; its ligand binds the {{c2::outside}} face and the G protein binds the {{c2::inside (cytosolic)}} loops. ||| GPCRs are seven-pass (7TM) receptors. Humans have over 700 of them, used for sight, smell and taste (all except sour). ~~~ Seven stitches through the fabric, and the G protein waits on the inside.
The trimeric G protein has three subunits: {{c1::Gα}}, which holds GDP or GTP, plus the {{c2::Gβγ}} pair. ||| Heterotrimeric means three different subunits. Gα and Gγ have lipid tails that anchor the trimer to the inner face of the membrane. ~~~ Alpha is the boss holding the switch; beta and gamma are the sidekicks that stick together.
When a ligand binds a GPCR, the receptor acts as a {{c1::GEF}}: it makes Gα release {{c2::GDP}} so {{c3::GTP}} can bind. ||| GTP is much more abundant in the cytosol, so once GDP leaves, GTP fills the empty pocket and Gα changes shape. ~~~ The receptor doesn't donate energy; it just opens the door so GTP can walk in.
With GTP bound, Gα {{c1::separates from Gβγ}}, and {{c2::either Gα-GTP or Gβγ}} can activate an effector protein. ||| Both halves can carry the signal. In the heart muscarinic example, it is Gβγ that opens the K+ channel. ~~~ Splitting the band: either member can go solo and still play the hit.
A G protein signal ends when Gα {{c1::hydrolyzes its own GTP to GDP + Pi}} and rejoins Gβγ. ||| Gα has a built-in GTPase that acts as a timer. Once GTP becomes GDP, Gα lets go of the effector and the trimer reforms. ~~~ GTP = Go Time Protein; hydrolysis is the timer running out.
The three main GPCR effector families are {{c1::adenylyl cyclase}}, {{c2::phospholipase C}} and {{c3::ion channels}}. ||| These are the three branches of Lecture 5: the cAMP branch, the IP3/DAG/Ca2+ branch, and G proteins that open channels directly. ~~~ A, P, I: Adenylyl cyclase, Phospholipase C, Ion channels, like an API with three endpoints.
Adenylyl cyclase converts {{c1::ATP}} into {{c2::cAMP}}, releasing pyrophosphate. ||| The reaction is pulled forward because pyrophosphate is immediately hydrolyzed. cAMP is a second messenger carrying the hormone's news inside. ~~~ Cyclase makes a cycle: it ties the leftover phosphate into a ring.
cAMP is destroyed by {{c1::cAMP phosphodiesterase (PDE)}}, which converts it to {{c2::5'-AMP}}. ||| This is the off switch for the cAMP branch. Blocking PDE keeps cAMP high and prolongs PKA activity. ~~~ PDE = Puts Down Everything: it breaks the cAMP ring so the message is gone.
Stimulatory hormones act through {{c1::Gαs}} to turn adenylyl cyclase on; inhibitory hormones like PGE1 and adenosine act through {{c2::Gαi}} to turn it down. ||| One cyclase can receive both inputs, and the cell sums them. ~~~ s for Stimulate, i for Inhibit.
An {{c1::agonist}} binds a receptor and mimics the hormone; an {{c2::antagonist}} occupies the receptor and blocks it. ||| Isoproterenol is a β-adrenergic agonist that relaxes bronchial muscle (asthma). Alprenolol is a β-blocker antagonist that slows the heart (arrhythmias). ~~~ Agonist Agrees with the hormone; Antagonist is the Anti guy blocking the door.
cAMP activates {{c1::protein kinase A (PKA)}} by binding its {{c2::regulatory (R)}} subunits, which release the active {{c3::catalytic (C)}} subunits. ||| Each R subunit has two cAMP sites, and binding is cooperative: the first cAMP makes the second bind more easily. That makes PKA a sharp switch. ~~~ R subunits are a parking brake; cAMP releases the brake and C drives away.
In liver and muscle, PKA {{c1::inactivates glycogen synthase}} and {{c2::activates glycogen phosphorylase kinase}}. ||| One signal stops glycogen storage and starts glycogen breakdown at the same time. Phosphorylation can switch an enzyme off as well as on. ~~~ Stop saving, start spending: fight-or-flight empties the sugar piggy bank.
Free PKA catalytic subunits enter the nucleus and phosphorylate the transcription factor {{c1::CREB}}, which turns on genes with a {{c2::CRE (cAMP response element)}}. ||| Phosphorylated CREB recruits CBP (CREB-binding protein). This is the slow, gene-level arm of cAMP signaling. ~~~ CREB = cAMP Response Element Binder; it reads its own name on the DNA.
Cholera toxin ADP-ribosylates {{c1::Gαs}} so it {{c2::can no longer hydrolyze GTP}}, keeping adenylyl cyclase on. ||| cAMP stays high in intestinal cells, which pump out chloride and water, causing massive diarrhea and dehydration. ~~~ Cholera glues the gas pedal down: Gαs can't let go of GTP.
Three ways a cAMP signal is terminated: hormone affinity {{c1::drops when Gα is GTP-bound}}, Gα {{c2::hydrolyzes GTP}}, and {{c3::PDE breaks down cAMP}}. ||| Nancy put these three on their own slide. Each is an off switch, and breaking any one prolongs the signal. ~~~ Receptor lets go, switch times out, messenger gets shredded.
Phospholipase C-β cleaves {{c1::PIP2}} into the second messengers {{c2::IP3}} and {{c3::DAG}}. ||| PIP2 is an inner-leaflet phospholipid made from PI by two kinase steps (PI to PI4P to PI(4,5)P2). PLC-β is the effector protein of Gαq. ~~~ PLC cuts the PIP2 sandwich into a floating head and a fatty tail.
{{c1::IP3}} diffuses to the ER and opens ligand-gated {{c2::Ca2+}} channels, while {{c3::DAG}} stays in the membrane and activates PKC. ||| IP3 is water-soluble, DAG is a lipid. PKC needs both DAG and Ca2+. In liver, PKC phosphorylates and inhibits glycogen synthase. ~~~ IP3 goes, DAG stays home.
Ca2+ binds {{c1::four}} sites on {{c2::calmodulin}}, which then activates targets such as CaM-kinase II and myosin light chain kinase. ||| Most Ca2+ effects go through calmodulin. Ca2+/calmodulin also activates cAMP phosphodiesterase, so Ca2+ can turn down the cAMP branch. ~~~ Calmodulin is the Ca2+ translator with four ears.
Once autophosphorylated, {{c1::CaM-kinase II}} stays partly active ({{c2::50-80%}}) even after Ca2+ and calmodulin leave. ||| It is a ring of 12 subunits whose neighbors phosphorylate each other. This gives the kinase a memory of past Ca2+ spikes. ~~~ CaMKII keeps a receipt of every calcium spike.
Cells end a Ca2+ signal with {{c1::Ca2+ pumps (ATP)}}, a {{c2::Na+/Ca2+ exchanger}}, Ca2+-binding proteins, and a {{c3::phosphatase that turns IP3 into IP2}}. ||| Cytosolic Ca2+ is kept around 10^-7 M versus about 10^-3 M outside, so small releases are big relative jumps. ~~~ Pump it, swap it, sponge it, and switch off the IP3 key.
The resting membrane potential of a typical animal cell is about {{c1::-70 mV}}, close to the {{c2::K+}} equilibrium potential. ||| Resting K+ channels let K+ leak out until the negative inside pulls back as hard as the gradient pushes. The Na+/K+ ATPase builds the K+ gradient. ~~~ K+ is King of the Kitchen (inside); the cell's voltage follows the king.
Inside a mammalian cell K+ is about {{c1::139 mM}} vs 4 mM outside, and Na+ is about {{c2::12 mM}} vs 145 mM outside. ||| These gradients set which way ions run when a channel opens: Na+ in, K+ out. ~~~ Potassium packs the inside, sodium is the salty sea outside.
The electrochemical gradient combines the {{c1::concentration gradient}} and the {{c2::membrane potential}} into one net driving force. ||| Opening a channel lets ions run downhill along this combined gradient; channels never pump. ~~~ Two hills stacked: one made of crowding, one made of charge.
In the heart, acetylcholine binds the {{c1::muscarinic}} receptor and {{c2::Gβγ}} opens K+ channels, hyperpolarizing the cell and {{c3::slowing}} the heartbeat. ||| Nancy's slide says the signal is carried by Gβγ, not Gα-GTP. K+ leaving makes the inside more negative. ~~~ Muscarinic = Mellow heart; beta-gamma opens the brake door.
Rhodopsin is opsin covalently linked to {{c1::11-cis-retinal}}, which a photon flips to {{c2::all-trans-retinal}}. ||| The shape change activates opsin, which activates the G protein transducin (Gt). Retinal comes from vitamin A; deficiency causes night blindness. ~~~ Light straightens the bent retinal (cis is bent, trans is straight).
Activated transducin turns on {{c1::cGMP phosphodiesterase}}, so cGMP {{c2::falls}} and cGMP-gated cation channels {{c3::close}}. ||| The rod hyperpolarizes and releases less neurotransmitter. Vision is the case where the second messenger goes down. ~~~ Light means less cGMP, channels shut, quiet rod.
In the dark, rod cells sit at about {{c1::-30 mV}} because {{c2::cGMP-gated channels are open}}, and they release neurotransmitter {{c3::constantly}}. ||| Na+ and Ca2+ leak in all the time. Light shuts this current off, which the brain reads as light. ~~~ Rods are night owls: chatty in the dark, silent in the light.
In smell, odorant receptors use {{c1::Gαolf}} and adenylyl cyclase 3 to raise {{c2::cAMP}}, which opens a {{c3::CNG}} channel. ||| Na+ and Ca2+ enter; Ca2+ opens a Cl- channel and Cl- leaves (these neurons keep Cl- high inside), adding more depolarization. Mice have about 1000 ORs, humans about 350. ~~~ Smell raises cAMP to open channels; vision lowers cGMP to close them.
Nitric oxide is made from {{c1::arginine}} by NO synthase in endothelial cells and activates {{c2::guanylyl cyclase}} in smooth muscle, making cGMP and relaxing the vessel. ||| NO is a gas, so it diffuses straight through membranes. The trigger is acetylcholine raising Ca2+ in the endothelium. ~~~ NO = Now Open the blood vessel.
# ---------- L6 Synapses ----------
{{c1::Afferent (sensory)}} neurons carry signals to the CNS, {{c2::efferent (motor)}} neurons carry them out to muscles and glands, and {{c3::interneurons}} connect the two inside the CNS. ||| Interneurons are the largest group. ~~~ Afferent Arrives, Efferent Exits.
An action potential is a rapid, transient, {{c1::all-or-none}} electrical spike in excitable cells. ||| It fires at full size or not at all. On her trace it rises from about -60 mV to about +50 mV and lasts a few milliseconds. ~~~ Like a gun: squeezing harder doesn't make a bigger bang.
Depolarization during an action potential is caused by opening of {{c1::voltage-gated Na+ channels}}; Na+ entry is very favorable because both the {{c2::concentration}} and {{c3::voltage}} gradients push it in. ||| Her slide gives ΔG of about -3.06 kcal/mol for Na+ moving in at -70 mV. ~~~ Two hills pointing the same way, so Na+ floods in.
The voltage-gated Na+ channel has three states: {{c1::closed}}, {{c2::open}}, and {{c3::inactivated}}. ||| Closed can open in response to depolarization. Inactivated is plugged by the inactivation segment and can't open (refractory period) until the membrane repolarizes. ~~~ COIN: Closed, Open, INactivated.
The positively charged {{c1::S4 helices}} act as the Na+ channel's voltage sensors. ||| Depolarization pushes them outward, which opens the channel in less than 0.1 ms. ~~~ S4 = Sensor 4 the voltage.
The membrane repolarizes because Na+ channels {{c1::inactivate}} and {{c2::voltage-gated (delayed) K+ channels}} open. ||| K+ channels respond more slowly, then stay open a bit too long, causing the hyperpolarizing undershoot. ~~~ Na+ lets the door slam shut; K+ arrives late and leaves the party last.
An action potential travels in one direction because the membrane behind it has Na+ channels in the {{c1::inactivated}} state. ||| The patch ahead has closed channels that can still open, so the spike only moves into fresh membrane. ~~~ Dominoes that take time to stand back up: the wave can only move toward standing ones.
Myelin insulates axons, and action potentials jump between gaps called {{c1::nodes of Ranvier}}, a process called {{c2::saltatory conduction}}. ||| Na+ channels are concentrated at the nodes, so the spike only regenerates there, which makes conduction much faster. ~~~ Hopscotch: skip the insulated squares, land on the nodes.
{{c1::Multiple sclerosis}} is immune destruction of CNS myelin; {{c2::Guillain-Barré syndrome}} is an immune attack on PNS myelin gangliosides. ||| Guillain-Barré often follows an infection and causes paralysis; it is a leading cause of non-trauma paralysis worldwide. ~~~ MS = Main System (CNS); GB = Goes to the Body (PNS).
Neurotransmitters are loaded into synaptic vesicles by an {{c1::H+ antiporter}}, powered by a {{c2::V-class H+ pump}}. ||| The pump fills the vesicle with protons, and the antiporter trades one H+ out for one transmitter in. ~~~ Protons pay the entry fee for each transmitter.
At the nerve terminal, the action potential opens {{c1::voltage-gated Ca2+ channels}}, and Ca2+ binding {{c2::synaptotagmin}} triggers vesicle fusion. ||| SNARE proteins (like v-SNARE VAMP) hold the docked vesicle to the membrane; botulinum toxin cuts SNAREs and blocks release. ~~~ Calcium is the send button.
Synaptic vesicles are recycled by {{c1::clathrin-mediated endocytosis}}; the fly {{c2::shibire}} mutation blocks dynamin and causes paralysis. ||| Transmitter is also cleared from the cleft by reuptake transporters or enzymes. ~~~ Shibire flies run out of vesicles, like a printer out of paper.
Excitatory neurotransmitters open {{c1::cation}} channels and depolarize; inhibitory ones open {{c2::K+ or Cl-}} channels and keep the cell from reaching threshold. ||| GABA and glycine are classic inhibitory transmitters; glutamate and ACh at muscle are excitatory. ~~~ Cations come in, cell gets excited; K+ out or Cl- in, cell calms down.
The acetylcholine receptor at the neuromuscular junction is a {{c1::nicotinic}} receptor, a {{c2::transmitter-gated cation channel}}. ||| It has five subunits and needs two ACh molecules (on the α subunits) to open. Contrast with the muscarinic GPCR in the heart. ~~~ Nicotinic = channel, muscarinic = GPCR: N before M, fast before slow.
The final channel in nerve-to-muscle signaling is the {{c1::Ca2+-release channel}} of the {{c2::sarcoplasmic reticulum}}, opened by voltage sensors in the {{c3::T tubules}}. ||| The full five: nerve terminal Ca2+ channel, nicotinic AChR, muscle Na+ channel, T-tubule Ca2+ channel, SR release channel. ~~~ The last door opens the calcium vault inside the muscle.
Contraction starts when Ca2+ binds {{c1::troponin}}, which moves {{c2::tropomyosin}} off the myosin-binding sites on actin. ||| Myosin heads then pull actin filaments toward the middle of the sarcomere (sliding filament model). Pumping Ca2+ back into the SR lets tropomyosin slide back. ~~~ Tropomyosin is the Do Not Touch rope; Ca2+ tells troponin to move it.
In the sliding filament model, {{c1::myosin}} pulls {{c2::actin}} filaments so the Z disks move closer, while the filaments themselves {{c3::stay the same length}}. ||| Sarcomeres run Z disk to Z disk; actin is anchored at the Z disks and myosin sits in the middle. ~~~ Two combs sliding into each other, teeth never shrink.
{{c1::Cocaine}} blocks the dopamine transporter, and {{c2::SSRIs like Prozac}} block serotonin reuptake, both prolonging signaling. ||| Blocking reuptake removes an off switch, so the transmitter stays in the cleft longer. ~~~ Reuptake blockers keep the music playing after the song should end.
{{c1::Sarin}} blocks acetylcholinesterase, while {{c2::curare}} blocks the muscle ACh receptor. ||| Sarin means too much ACh signaling (cleanup is gone); curare means none (receptor blocked, paralysis). ~~~ Sarin jams the off switch; curare unplugs the receiver.
Valium is a {{c1::positive allosteric modulator}} of the GABA receptor, a ligand-gated {{c2::Cl-}} channel, so neurons {{c3::hyperpolarize}} and fire less. ||| It helps GABA bind, more Cl- enters, and the gap between resting potential and threshold grows. ~~~ Valium turns up GABA's volume knob.
# ---------- L7 Optogenetics ----------
Optogenetics uses {{c1::light}} plus a {{c2::gene for a light-sensitive protein}} to switch specific neurons on or off. ||| Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel received the 2026 Nobel Prize (per her slide) for the discoveries behind it. ~~~ Opto (light) + genetics (the gene you insert).
Channelrhodopsins were discovered in the green alga {{c1::Chlamydomonas reinhardtii}}, which uses its {{c2::eyespot}} for phototaxis. ||| The alga swims toward light to photosynthesize. It was already a model organism for cilia and intraflagellar transport. ~~~ The pond algae with an orange eye gave us the light switch.
Channelrhodopsin-1 was found to conduct {{c1::protons (H+)}}, while channelrhodopsin-2 is a {{c2::non-selective cation channel}} (Na+, K+, Ca2+). ||| ChR1 was expressed in Xenopus oocytes (2002). ChR2 (2003) passes Na+, which is what neurons use to fire, and it worked in mammalian cells. ~~~ ChR2 is the 2-for-neurons version: it lets Na+ in.
ChR2 opens in response to {{c1::blue light (about 470 nm)}}, when its retinal flips from {{c2::all-trans}} to {{c3::13-cis}}. ||| It is a 7TM protein covalently linked to retinal, but it is the channel itself; no G protein or second messenger, so it is fast. ~~~ Blue for go.
The 2005 Boyden et al. paper showed ChR2 in cultured neurons fires action potentials with {{c1::millisecond}} precision. ||| One blue pulse, one spike; no light, no spikes. This is considered the birth of optogenetics. ~~~ Each flash is a trigger pull.
To use optogenetics in living animals, genes were delivered with {{c1::harmless viruses (lentivirus or AAV)}} and light with an implanted {{c2::fiber-optic cable}}. ||| A cell-type-specific promoter decides which neurons make the protein. Transgenic mice are another way to express it. ~~~ A virus delivery truck for the gene, a fiber-optic straw for the light.
In 2007, blue light on {{c1::hypocretin (orexin)}} neurons in the hypothalamus {{c2::woke sleeping mice}}. ||| This was the first in vivo proof that optogenetics could control behavior. ~~~ Orexin is the alarm clock neuron.
Activating ChR2-expressing neurons in the {{c1::subfornical organ (SFO)}} made fully hydrated mice {{c2::drink large amounts of water}}. ||| ChR2 was tagged with YFP to see expression. Non-infected mice given light didn't drink, which is the control that rules out light itself. ~~~ Flip the thirst switch and the mouse heads for the water bottle.
{{c1::Halorhodopsin}} is a light-driven pump that moves {{c2::Cl- into}} the cell, silencing neurons. ||| Cl- (negative) entering makes the inside more negative, so the neuron hyperpolarizes. It comes from archaebacteria. ~~~ Halo puts a Cl- halo inside and Holds the neuron quiet.
{{c1::Bacteriorhodopsin}} pumps {{c2::H+ out}} of the cell in response to light, which inhibits neurons. ||| Removing positive charge hyperpolarizes the membrane. Pumps can move ions against their gradient; channels can't. ~~~ BR = Bye, protons.
{{c1::OptoXR}} tools are rhodopsins engineered to activate chosen {{c2::Gα proteins}}, controlling second messengers with light. ||| They change cAMP or IP3 levels instead of directly changing membrane voltage. ~~~ OptoXR = light-run GPCR remote.
# ---------- L8 RTKs ----------
An RTK has an extracellular ligand-binding domain, {{c1::a single}} transmembrane helix, and a cytoplasmic {{c2::tyrosine kinase}} domain. ||| Humans have over 60 RTK genes in about 20 subfamilies. Compare GPCRs, which cross seven times and have no enzyme activity. ~~~ GPCR = 7 stitches, no engine; RTK = 1 stitch with an engine attached.
Ligand binding causes RTKs to {{c1::dimerize}}, and the kinase domains {{c2::cross-phosphorylate}} each other's activation lip. ||| This raises kinase activity, and the kinases then phosphorylate more tyrosines on the receptor tails. ~~~ Pair up, poke each other, put out landing pads.
FGF receptor activation requires {{c1::heparan sulfate}}, which binds FGF and helps form the 2:2 ligand-receptor dimer. ||| Many RTKs dimerize this way. ~~~ Heparan sulfate is the glue on the handshake.
The EGF receptor forms an {{c1::asymmetric}} kinase dimer in which an {{c2::activator}} kinase switches on a {{c3::receiver}} kinase. ||| It is a lopsided activation rather than symmetric cross-phosphorylation. ~~~ One kinase gives the other a push start.
Phosphotyrosines on activated RTKs are docking sites for proteins with {{c1::SH2}} domains (and less commonly {{c2::PTB}} domains). ||| SH2 is about 100 amino acids; humans have 115 SH2 proteins. PTB domains usually bind Asn-Pro-X-Tyr motifs. ~~~ P-Tyr is Velcro; SH2 is the matching Velcro strip.
RTK signaling is turned off by {{c1::receptor-mediated endocytosis}} and {{c2::lysosomal degradation}}, promoted by mono-ubiquitination by {{c3::c-Cbl}}. ||| After EGF binds, HER1 endocytosis increases about 10-fold. Kinase-dead mutants aren't endocytosed faster. ~~~ The receptor that shouts gets eaten.
Endocytosis can also sustain signaling: {{c1::NGF}}-receptor complexes are carried in vesicles from axon tips to the cell body as a {{c2::survival}} signal. ||| This is the exception to endocytosis as an off switch. ~~~ A care package mailed back to the cell body.
Ras is a {{c1::small monomeric GTPase}} anchored to the plasma membrane by {{c2::lipid groups}}. ||| Ras-GTP is ON, Ras-GDP is OFF. Almost all RTKs can activate the Ras/MAP kinase pathway. ~~~ Ras is a one-piece G protein, unlike the three-piece trimer.
A {{c1::GEF}} activates Ras by swapping GDP for GTP; a {{c2::GAP}} inactivates it by speeding GTP hydrolysis. ||| Sos is the Ras-GEF downstream of RTKs. NF1 is a Ras-GAP. ~~~ GEF = Gets Engine Fired; GAP = Gives Ability to Pause.
The adaptor {{c1::GRB2}} binds receptor phosphotyrosines with its {{c2::SH2}} domain and holds Sos with its {{c3::SH3}} domains. ||| Docking GRB2 drags Sos to the membrane next to Ras, so Sos can activate Ras. ~~~ GRaB 2: one hand grabs the receptor, the other hands grab Sos.
Constitutively active RasD usually carries a mutation at {{c1::glycine 12}}, which blocks {{c2::GAP}} so Ras stays GTP-bound. ||| Any amino acid except proline at position 12 does this. About 30% of human tumors have hyperactive Ras. ~~~ G12 is the glitch that jams the off switch.
Antibodies against Ras or a {{c1::dominant negative}} Ras block growth-factor-induced proliferation, while {{c2::constitutively active}} Ras causes proliferation without growth factor. ||| Together these show Ras is required and sufficient downstream of RTKs. ~~~ Block it and growth stops; lock it on and growth never stops.
In the fly eye, the R7 precursor carries the RTK {{c1::Sevenless}}, which is activated by {{c2::Boss}} on the {{c3::R8}} cell. ||| Boss is a 7-pass membrane protein, so this is contact-dependent signaling. Without the signal, the precursor becomes a cone cell. ~~~ Sevenless lacks R7; Boss is the Bride who must touch Sevenless.
A sevenless mutant expressing {{c1::RasD}} still forms R7, showing Ras acts {{c2::downstream}} of Sev. ||| Epistasis: when an ON mutation rescues an OFF mutation, the ON gene is downstream. ~~~ Hot-wiring the car skips the broken ignition key.
Downstream Sev pathway genes were found using {{c1::enhancer and suppressor}} screens in flies with a {{c2::temperature-sensitive}} Sev. ||| Simply looking for flies missing R7 failed because loss of downstream genes is lethal; dead flies don't make eyes. ~~~ Tune the receptor to half-power and look for mutations that tip the balance.
FRET gives a signal only when two fluorescent labels are {{c1::very close (bound)}}; it showed Ras becoming {{c2::GTP-bound}} within minutes of EGF. ||| Ras-YFP excited at 476 nm transfers energy to a red-labeled Ras-GTP binding protein, emitting at 617 nm instead of 528 nm. ~~~ FRET is a molecular ruler: color change means touching.
The MAP kinase module downstream of Ras is {{c1::Raf}} (MAPKKK), then {{c2::MEK}} (MAPKK), then {{c3::ERK}} (MAPK). ||| Each kinase phosphorylates the next. Constitutively active Raf drives proliferation; nonfunctional Raf blocks it even with RasD. ~~~ Ras Rarely Makes Errors: Ras, Raf, MEK, ERK.
Growth factors rapidly induce about 100 {{c1::early response genes}}, including {{c2::c-fos}}, whose enhancer contains a {{c3::serum response element (SRE)}}. ||| ERK phosphorylates TCF, which sits with SRF on the SRE. c-fos is needed for cell cycle progression. ~~~ c-fos is the first alarm the growth signal rings in the nucleus.
{{c1::Scaffold proteins}} hold the kinases of one MAPK module together to prevent {{c2::crosstalk}} between modules that share components. ||| Yeast has at least six MAPK modules; mammals have at least five using 12 MAPKs, 7 MAPKKs and 7 MAPKKKs. ~~~ A tool belt keeps each pathway's tools together.
Some RTKs activate {{c1::Rho family GTPases}} to remodel the actin cytoskeleton, which is used in {{c2::axon guidance}}. ||| Cdc42 makes filopodia, Rac makes lamellipodia, Rho makes stress fibers. ~~~ Same GTPase trick, but the output is cell shape instead of genes.
Growth cones have {{c1::filopodia}} (bundled actin spikes) and {{c2::lamellipodia}} (sheets of cross-linked actin mesh). ||| The growth cone is the steering tip of a growing axon. ~~~ Filopodia are fingers, lamellipodia are the palm.
Posterior retinal axons avoid posterior tectum because posterior tectum is {{c1::repulsive}}, not because anterior tectum is attractive. ||| Nancy wrote this sentence on her slide. It is a classic trap. ~~~ They're running from something, not toward something.
Posterior retinal neurons express high {{c1::EphA}}, and the tectum has a gradient of {{c2::ephrinA}} that is {{c3::highest posteriorly}}. ||| Without the receptor, axons are no longer repelled from posterior tectum. This builds the retinotopic map. ~~~ Eph = Eww, push away.
EphrinAs are {{c1::GPI-anchored}} and bind EphA receptors; ephrinBs are {{c2::transmembrane}} proteins that bind EphB receptors and can do reverse signaling. ||| Eph receptors are the largest RTK family (13 in humans; 8 EphA, 5 EphB). ~~~ A is Anchored by GPI; B goes Both ways through the membrane.
EphA4 causes growth cone collapse through the Rho GEF {{c1::Ephexin}}, which activates {{c2::RhoA}}. ||| A dominant negative Ephexin blocked ephrinA-induced collapse, showing Ephexin is required. ~~~ Ephexin hexes the growth cone into collapsing.
# ---------- L9 Cancer I ----------
Cancer cells are defined by {{c1::uncontrolled proliferation}} and the ability to {{c2::invade and colonize other tissues (metastasis)}}. ||| A tumor can form from more division, or from less apoptosis with normal division. ~~~ Grow without limits, then move out.
A {{c1::benign}} tumor stays in one local mass in its tissue; a {{c2::malignant}} tumor invades through the {{c3::basal lamina}} and can metastasize. ||| Invasiveness is the essential feature of cancer, and metastases usually kill patients. ~~~ Benign Behaves and stays home; Malignant Moves out.
{{c1::Carcinomas}} arise from epithelial cells and make up about {{c2::80%}} of human cancers because epithelia {{c3::divide often}}. ||| Sarcomas come from connective tissue or muscle; leukemias and lymphomas from blood-forming cells. ~~~ Cancer needs dividing cells, and linings divide the most.
An adenoma is a benign glandular epithelial tumor; its malignant form is an {{c1::adenocarcinoma}}. A chondroma's malignant form is a {{c2::chondrosarcoma}}. ||| Carcin means epithelial origin, sarc means connective tissue origin. ~~~ Plain -oma behaves; add carcin or sarc and it bites.
Unlike normal cells, cancer cells in culture {{c1::pile up in foci}}, divide {{c2::indefinitely}}, and grow {{c3::without growth factors}}. ||| Normal cells form a monolayer, stop when touching (contact inhibition), and respond to inhibitory signals. ~~~ Normal cells mind their personal space; cancer cells stack like a mosh pit.
In the {{c1::Warburg effect}}, cancer cells take up lots of glucose and use glycolysis mainly to make {{c2::building blocks}}, sending little through oxidative phosphorylation. ||| PET scans detect tumors with radioactive fluorodeoxyglucose. This happens even when oxygen is present. ~~~ Cancer eats fast food for the raw materials, not the energy.
Mutant {{c1::isocitrate dehydrogenase}} makes the new metabolite {{c2::2-hydroxyglutarate}}, which blocks enzymes that need α-ketoglutarate. ||| Found in about 70% of certain brain cancers and about 25% of AML. Many of the blocked enzymes control gene expression. ~~~ A counterfeit key jams every α-KG lock.
Telomeres are {{c1::TTAGGG}} repeats that shorten each division unless {{c2::telomerase}} adds them back. ||| Most somatic cells make little telomerase, so they stop dividing (replicative senescence); fibroblasts stop after 25-50 doublings. Cancer cells keep telomerase on. ~~~ Telomeres are shoelace tips; telomerase is the repair kit cancer won't give up.
CML cells all share the same 9;22 translocation breakpoint (the {{c1::Philadelphia chromosome}}), evidence for {{c2::clonal origin}} from one cell. ||| Clonal origin means a primary tumor descends from a single cell with a heritable change. One mutation still isn't enough for cancer. ~~~ Same fingerprint on every cell, so one ancestor.
The {{c1::multi-hit}} model says cancer needs several mutations accumulating in one cell lineage over years. ||| Support: cancer rises steeply with age, myc + rasD mice get tumors faster than either alone, and colon cancer shows stepwise mutations. ~~~ A car needs several failures at once to crash.
In colon cancer, the usual mutation order is {{c1::APC}}, then {{c2::K-ras}}, then loss of {{c3::Smad4}} (chromosome 18q, the old "DCC region"), then {{c4::p53}}. ||| APC loss starts a polyp, K-ras grows the adenoma, Smad4 loss removes the TGFβ brake, and p53 loss comes late at the step to carcinoma. Lecture 11's updated figure names Smad4 for the chromosome 18 step. ~~~ A Kid Should Panic: APC, K-ras, Smad4, p53.
Cancer cells are {{c1::genetically unstable}}, often from mutations in {{c2::DNA maintenance (caretaker)}} genes. ||| Unstable genomes gather mutations faster and produce heterogeneous cells within one tumor. ~~~ Fire the mechanic and every other part breaks faster.
